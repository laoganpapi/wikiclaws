# 1. Legal Framework: The Genocide Convention Standard

*Doctrine only. This section does not apply the standard to any conflict. Current through May 25, 2026.*

## 1. Convention text

The Genocide Convention was adopted 9 December 1948 and entered into force 12 January 1951 (UN GA Res. 260 A (III)).

Article II provides: "In the present Convention, genocide means any of the following acts committed with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such: (a) Killing members of the group; (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm to members of the group; (c) Deliberately inflicting on the group conditions of life calculated to bring about its physical destruction in whole or in part; (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group; (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group" (Genocide Convention, Art. II, 1948).

Article III enumerates the punishable acts: genocide; conspiracy; direct and public incitement; attempt; and complicity (Genocide Convention, Art. III, 1948). Article IV provides that "Persons committing genocide or any of the other acts enumerated in Article III shall be punished, whether they are constitutionally responsible rulers, public officials or private individuals" (Art. IV). The ICJ has confirmed the obligations are *erga omnes partes*: every State party has standing to invoke another State's responsibility for breach (ICJ, *Gambia v. Myanmar*, Judgment on Preliminary Objections, 22 July 2022, ¶¶107–114).

The Rome Statute reproduces Article II in Article 6 (Rome Statute, Art. 6, 17 July 1998). The ICC Elements of Crimes add a contextual requirement: "The conduct took place in the context of a manifest pattern of similar conduct directed against that group or was conduct that could itself effect such destruction" (ICC, Elements of Crimes, Art. 6 (a)–(e), element 4). Scholars dispute whether this contextual element imports a substantive requirement absent from the Convention or merely operationalises the mens rea inquiry (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, ch. 6; Ambos, *Int'l Rev. Red Cross* 91 (876), 2009).

## 2. The five acts

### (a) Killing members of the group

The ICTR and ICTY have read "killing" as requiring intentional unlawful homicide rather than mere causation of death (ICTR, *Akayesu*, Trial Judgment, 2 Sept 1998, ¶¶500–501; ICTY, *Krstić*, Appeals Judgment, 19 April 2004, ¶34). The ICJ in *Bosnia* accepted the Srebrenica killings as fulfilling Article II (a) (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, Judgment, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶276–297).

### (b) Causing serious bodily or mental harm

In *Akayesu*, the ICTR defined this as "bodily or mental torture, inhumane or degrading treatment, persecution," and held that rape and sexual violence can constitute serious bodily and mental harm where committed with the requisite intent (ICTR, *Akayesu*, Trial Judgment, 2 Sept 1998, ¶¶504, 731–734). Later jurisprudence clarified that the harm need not be permanent or irremediable but must be serious enough to contribute to the destruction of the group in whole or in part (ICTR, *Kayishema and Ruzindana*, Trial Judgment, 21 May 1999, ¶¶108–113; ICTY, *Krstić*, Trial Judgment, 2 Aug 2001, ¶513). The ICJ adopted this threshold in *Bosnia* (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶300–319).

### (c) Deliberately inflicting conditions of life calculated to bring about physical destruction

The Akayesu Chamber identified illustrative methods: "subjecting a group of people to a subsistence diet, systematic expulsion from homes and the reduction of essential medical services below minimum requirement" (ICTR, *Akayesu*, Trial Judgment, 2 Sept 1998, ¶¶505–506). The ICC Elements of Crimes specify that "conditions of life" may include "deliberate deprivation of resources indispensable for survival, such as food or medical services, or systematic expulsion from homes" (ICC, Elements of Crimes, Art. 6 (c), n. 4). The conduct need not have actually produced physical destruction at the time of charge; it suffices that the conditions are calculated to do so (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, pp. 191–197).

### (d) Imposing measures intended to prevent births within the group

The ICTR held that measures preventing births include sexual mutilation, sterilisation, forced birth control, separation of the sexes, and prohibition of marriages, and may extend to rape where the perpetrator's intent is to prevent the victim from procreating within the group (ICTR, *Akayesu*, Trial Judgment, 2 Sept 1998, ¶¶507–508). Birth-prevention measures may be mental as well as physical (e.g. trauma deterring procreation) (ICTR, *Akayesu*, ¶508).

### (e) Forcibly transferring children of the group to another group

The ICC Elements of Crimes require that the transferred persons be under 18, that the perpetrator knew or should have known they were under 18, and that the transfer be by force, threat of force, coercion, or comparable means (ICC, Elements of Crimes, Art. 6 (e)). No ICJ judgment has found this element established on the merits.

## 3. Mens rea: dolus specialis

Article II requires that the listed acts be committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a [protected] group, as such." The ICJ describes this as *dolus specialis*: a specific intent distinct from the intent required for the underlying acts (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, Judgment, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶186–189). Discriminatory motive (targeting members because of their group membership) is insufficient on its own; the perpetrator must intend the destruction of the group as such (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶187).

Where direct evidence of intent is absent, intent may be inferred from a pattern of conduct, but only on a stringent threshold: "for a pattern of conduct to be accepted as evidence of [genocidal intent], it would have to be such that it could only point to the existence of such intent" (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶373). The Court restated the formulation in *Croatia v. Serbia*: "in order to infer the existence of *dolus specialis* from a pattern of conduct, it is necessary and sufficient that this is the only inference that could reasonably be drawn from the acts in question" (ICJ, *Croatia v. Serbia*, Judgment, 3 Feb 2015, ¶148; see also ¶¶417, 440). In *Croatia*, the Court rejected the genocide claim because forced displacement was at least an equally plausible inference (ICJ, *Croatia v. Serbia*, 3 Feb 2015, ¶¶437–441).

The intent requirement distinguishes genocide from other international crimes:

- **Ethnic cleansing.** Forced displacement is not itself genocide; it may evidence genocidal intent only where physical or biological destruction is the only reasonable inference (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶190; *Croatia v. Serbia*, 3 Feb 2015, ¶162).
- **Crimes against humanity.** Persecution and extermination require widespread or systematic attack on a civilian population but no intent to destroy a protected group as such (Rome Statute, Art. 7; ICTY, *Krstić*, Appeals Judgment, 19 April 2004, ¶¶223–227).
- **War crimes.** Grave violations of IHL in armed conflict; no group-destruction intent required (Rome Statute, Art. 8).

Scholars dispute whether "intent to destroy" requires a purpose-based mental state (the perpetrator specifically desires destruction) or whether a knowledge-based mental state suffices (the perpetrator knows destruction is the manifest result of a campaign). Greenawalt argued for a knowledge-based reading (Greenawalt, "Rethinking Genocidal Intent: The Case for a Knowledge-Based Interpretation," *Columbia Law Review* 99, 1999). Ambos distinguishes the leadership level (purpose) from lower-level perpetrators (knowledge of the genocidal context) (Ambos, *Int'l Rev. Red Cross* 91 (876), 2009). Schabas, while defending a purpose-based reading, has acknowledged the force of the knowledge-based approach (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, ch. 5). The ICJ has not endorsed the knowledge-based reading; its formulations in *Bosnia* and *Croatia* track a purpose-based conception (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶187, 373; *Croatia v. Serbia*, 3 Feb 2015, ¶148).

## 4. Protected group

Article II lists four protected categories: national, ethnical, racial, or religious groups. Political, social, and economic groups are excluded by the drafters' design (UN OLA, *travaux préparatoires*, UN Doc. A/C.6/SR.69, 1948; Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, pp. 117–171).

Group identification has generated doctrinal debate. The Akayesu Chamber initially suggested an objective approach (a "stable and permanent" group identifiable by birth) (ICTR, *Akayesu*, Trial Judgment, 2 Sept 1998, ¶¶510–516). Later ICTR and ICTY decisions moved toward a mixed objective–subjective standard, asking how the perpetrator perceived the targeted collectivity (ICTY, *Krstić*, Trial Judgment, 2 Aug 2001, ¶¶555–557; ICTR, *Rutaganda*, Trial Judgment, 6 Dec 1999, ¶¶55–58). Schabas argues that the four categories must retain objective content lest the requirement collapse into the perpetrator's discretion (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, pp. 124–129).

In *South Africa v. Israel*, the ICJ observed at the provisional-measures stage that "the Palestinians appear to constitute a distinct 'national, ethnical, racial or religious group', and hence a protected group within the meaning of Article II of the Genocide Convention," and that Palestinians in Gaza form a substantial part of that group (ICJ, *South Africa v. Israel*, Order on Provisional Measures, 26 Jan 2024, ¶45). The Court characterised that finding as part of the plausibility assessment, not a merits determination (ICJ, *South Africa v. Israel*, 26 Jan 2024, ¶¶30, 45–54).

## 5. "In whole or in part"

The Krstić Appeals Chamber set the controlling test: where conviction rests on intent to destroy the group "in part," "the part must be a substantial part of that group" (ICTY, *Krstić*, Appeals Judgment, 19 April 2004, ¶8). The Convention's object being the protection of human groups from mass crimes, "the intent to destroy must target at least a substantial part of the group" (*Krstić*, ¶¶8–12).

Substantiality is assessed on non-exclusive criteria: (i) numeric size of the targeted part relative to the whole; (ii) prominence within the larger group (e.g. leadership, military-age men, intellectual elite); (iii) geographic concentration within the perpetrator's area of activity; and (iv) emblematic significance (*Krstić*, ¶¶12–14). The Srebrenica genocide rested on the destruction of approximately 7,000–8,000 Bosnian Muslim men and boys; the Appeals Chamber accepted that this group, though numerically small, was a substantial part because of its emblematic and strategic significance and its geographic concentration (*Krstić*, ¶¶15–22). The ICJ endorsed the framework in *Bosnia v. Serbia* (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶198–201).

## 6. State responsibility vs. individual criminal responsibility

The Convention establishes two parallel regimes:

- **State responsibility.** Article IX confers ICJ jurisdiction over disputes relating to "the responsibility of a State for genocide" (Genocide Convention, Art. IX). The ICJ adjudicates whether a State has breached its obligations, including the obligations not to commit, to prevent, and to punish genocide (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶161–169, 425–450). State responsibility does not require identification of a specific natural person but requires attribution to the State (ILC, *Articles on Responsibility of States for Internationally Wrongful Acts*, 2001, Arts. 4–11; ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, ¶¶379–415).

- **Individual criminal responsibility.** Article IV provides that natural persons are punishable. Individual liability is adjudicated by domestic courts, ad hoc tribunals (ICTY, ICTR, IRMCT), or the ICC under Rome Statute Article 6 (Rome Statute, Arts. 6, 25). Modes of liability include direct perpetration, co-perpetration, indirect perpetration, ordering, instigation, and aiding and abetting (Rome Statute, Art. 25(3)).

The ICJ may order cessation, non-repetition, reparation, and provisional measures (ICJ Statute, Art. 41; ILC Articles, Arts. 30–37). The ICC imposes individual criminal sanctions including imprisonment (Rome Statute, Arts. 77–78). State responsibility and individual liability can coexist (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶173–179).

## 7. Plausibility standard at provisional measures

Provisional measures under Article 41 of the ICJ Statute require that the rights asserted be "plausible" and that there be a link between those rights and the measures requested (ICJ, *Gambia v. Myanmar*, Order on Provisional Measures, 23 Jan 2020, ¶¶43–58, 74).

In *Gambia v. Myanmar*, the Court rejected the argument that plausibility required preliminary evidence of genocidal intent. It held it was sufficient that the alleged acts "appear capable of falling within the provisions of the Convention," and that "in view of the function of provisional measures" the Court was not required to determine the existence of genocidal intent at that stage (ICJ, *Gambia v. Myanmar*, 23 Jan 2020, ¶¶30, 56).

The Court applied the same framework in *South Africa v. Israel*, finding that "at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa and for which it is seeking protection are plausible" (ICJ, *South Africa v. Israel*, Order on Provisional Measures, 26 Jan 2024, ¶54). The Court was explicit that this was not a merits determination: "the Court is not called upon at this stage of the proceedings to determine definitively whether Israel's obligations under the Genocide Convention have been violated" (ICJ, *South Africa v. Israel*, 26 Jan 2024, ¶30). Judge Sebutinde, dissenting, characterised the dispute as a political conflict outside the Convention's scope (Sebutinde, Dissenting Opinion, 26 Jan 2024). The Court issued further measures on 28 March 2024 (humanitarian assistance) and 24 May 2024 (halt to Rafah operations that could inflict destructive conditions of life) (ICJ, *South Africa v. Israel*, Orders of 28 March 2024 and 24 May 2024).

As of May 25, 2026, the merits stage in *South Africa v. Israel* has not been adjudicated. South Africa filed its memorial on 28 October 2024; Israel filed its counter-memorial on 12 March 2026. On 12 March 2026, Namibia, the United States, Hungary, and Fiji filed declarations of intervention under Article 63 of the ICJ Statute (ICJ Press Releases, *South Africa v. Israel* docket, accessed May 2026).

## 8. Standard of proof on the merits

The ICJ applies a heightened evidentiary standard for genocide claims on the merits. In *Bosnia v. Serbia*, the Court held: "claims against a State involving charges of exceptional gravity must be proved by evidence that is fully conclusive" (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, Judgment, 26 Feb 2007, ¶209). The Court reaffirmed this in *Croatia v. Serbia*: allegations of genocide require "fully conclusive" evidence, and the same standard applies to the underlying acts and to the inference of intent (ICJ, *Croatia v. Serbia*, Judgment, 3 Feb 2015, ¶178).

"Fully conclusive" is the highest standard the ICJ has articulated in inter-State proceedings, higher than "balance of probabilities" and exceeding "clear and convincing evidence" (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, pp. 510–520; Mettraux, *International Crimes: Law and Practice*, vol. I, 2019, ch. 7). The standard applies cumulatively to (i) the underlying *actus reus* (whether the listed acts occurred and are attributable to the respondent State) and (ii) the *dolus specialis* (whether the only reasonable inference from the pattern is genocidal intent) (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶¶209, 373; *Croatia v. Serbia*, 3 Feb 2015, ¶¶178, 148).

Individual criminal proceedings apply different standards. ICC conviction requires beyond reasonable doubt (Rome Statute, Art. 66(3)); confirmation of charges requires "substantial grounds to believe" (Art. 61(7)); arrest warrants require "reasonable grounds to believe" (Art. 58(1)(a)). Scholars dispute whether "fully conclusive" is materially more demanding than beyond-reasonable-doubt or merely the same threshold in different language (Mettraux, *International Crimes*, vol. I, 2019, pp. 290–305).

Two doctrinal points follow:

- **Inference from pattern of conduct.** Direct evidence of genocidal intent being rare, the "only reasonable inference" formulation bridges circumstantial pattern evidence and the fully conclusive standard (ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, 26 Feb 2007, ¶373; *Croatia v. Serbia*, 3 Feb 2015, ¶148). The pattern must exclude alternative reasonable inferences (e.g. military objective, forced displacement, retaliation) (*Croatia v. Serbia*, ¶¶437–441).
- **Attribution.** For State responsibility, conduct must be attributable under the customary rules codified in the ILC Articles (organ status, effective control, acknowledgement) (ILC Articles, 2001, Arts. 4–11; ICJ, *Bosnia v. Serbia*, ¶¶379–415). Conduct of non-State actors is attributable only on a strict showing.
