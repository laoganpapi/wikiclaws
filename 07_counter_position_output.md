# Counter-Position: The Case Against the Genocide Characterization

*Sub-Agent 7 input to* Application of the Genocide Convention to the Israel–Palestine Conflict: An Evidentiary Review (as of May 2026)*. This catalogue records the legal and factual case against the genocide characterization at its strongest. It does not endorse that case. It records arguments as arguments; the ICJ will adjudicate the merits.*

## 1. Israel's legal argument at the ICJ

Israel's defense has unfolded in three filings: oral argument at the provisional measures phase on January 12, 2024; further written observations on subsequent South African requests; and the counter-memorial filed March 12, 2026, after two extensions of the original July 2025 deadline (ICJ Case 192 docket; Anadolu Agency, "ICJ grants Israel another extension," 2025). Pleadings remain largely confidential pending oral hearings; the public structure of the argument is consistent across the January 2024 oral round, MFA statements, and the March 2026 filing notice.

**(a) War aim is destruction of Hamas, not of Palestinians.** Opening for Israel on January 12, 2024, MFA Legal Adviser Tal Becker framed the operation as self-defense against an armed group whose stated objective was Israel's destruction and called South Africa's case "a libel" intended to "deny Israel the right to defend itself" (Becker, CR 2024/2, 12 January 2024). On the counter-memorial, the Israeli MFA said Israel "has submitted its Counter-Memorial to the ICJ, rejecting South Africa's fabricated and politicized blood libel" and that "the court lacks jurisdiction, and South Africa's allegations should be dismissed in their entirety" (Israel MFA, 14 March 2026; *Daily Maverick*, 16 March 2026).

**(b) UN Charter Article 51 self-defense.** Israel argues the operation is a lawful response to the October 7 armed attack and is exercised under Article 51 for hostage recovery (Becker, CR 2024/2; Israel MFA releases). The Court did not rule on Article 51 at the provisional measures stage.

**(c) Dual-use targeting under IHL distinction.** Galit Raguan and Christopher Staker argued that Hamas's embedding inside hospitals, schools, mosques, and residential buildings converts those sites to lawful military objectives once used to make an effective contribution to military action. Staker: "The inevitable fatalities and human suffering of any conflict is not of itself a pattern of conduct that plausibly shows genocidal intent" (Staker, CR 2024/2; Al Jazeera, "ICJ genocide case: What are Israel's arguments?", 12 January 2024).

**(d) Evacuation warnings, corridors, aid.** Raguan walked the Court through IDF evacuation notices, leaflet drops, telephone warnings, and humanitarian corridors as positive evidence that intent was not to destroy the group as such (Raguan, CR 2024/2; IDF Spokesperson statements). South Africa, OCHA, and others contest operational adequacy; Israel cites their existence as inconsistent with Article II specific intent.

**(e) Treatment of officials' statements.** Israel argued that statements South Africa relies on (Defense Minister Gallant's October 9, 2023 "no electricity, no food, no water, no fuel" remark; PM Netanyahu's "Amalek" reference; comments by Minister Smotrich and others) are either (i) rhetorical and non-operational, (ii) made by figures outside the military chain of command, (iii) extracted from longer statements in which Israel reaffirmed IHL compliance, or (iv) contradicted by Cabinet decisions and IDF rules of engagement (Becker and Noam, CR 2024/2; *Haaretz*, "Israel Pushes Back at ICJ Gaza Genocide Charge," 11 January 2024). Israel invokes *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015) for the proposition that statements must be evaluated against actual state conduct.

**(f) Hamas shielding doctrine.** Civilian harm caused by an attacker against a target shielded by the defending force does not transfer mens rea to the attacker for genocide purposes, and documented Hamas use of civilian infrastructure shifts material responsibility (Noam, CR 2024/2).

**(g) Plausibility as procedural, not merits.** Israel reads the January 26, 2024 order as finding only that some rights asserted by South Africa were "plausible" for jurisdictional purposes, and notes that the Court expressly did not rule on whether genocide is occurring (ICJ Order of 26 January 2024, paras. 30, 54, 74; Israel MFA, 26 January 2024). Scholars critical of Israel's broader position have agreed with this narrow reading (Heller, Opinio Juris; Akande and Milanovic, EJIL: Talk!, January–February 2024).

**(h) Domestic legal oversight.** Israel cites the Military Advocate General, the Supreme Court, and the Attorney General as functioning oversight institutions. The record is contested. The IDF MAG opened investigations into Sde Teiman detainee abuse (CNN, "Major General Yifat Tomer Yerushalmi," 4 November 2025), but the MAG resigned in late 2025 amid politicized backlash, and on March 12, 2026 her successor withdrew the criminal charges against five reservists (Israel Democracy Institute, "End of the Sde Teiman Abuse Case," 2026). Critics argue the sequence undercuts the oversight argument; Israel argues the existence of investigation, prosecution, and judicial review is itself probative of non-genocidal intent.

## 2. The IHL framework Israel invokes

Israel's substantive legal position rests on the proposition that civilian harm in dense urban combat against an armed group embedded in civilian infrastructure is governed by IHL (distinction, proportionality, precautions) and not by the Genocide Convention's specific-intent test. The argument has two prongs.

First, the legal categories are distinct. War crimes punish unlawful conduct in hostilities. Crimes against humanity punish widespread or systematic attack on civilians. Genocide requires the additional, narrow mens rea of intent to destroy a protected group as such. Conflating IHL violations with genocide, on this view, collapses categories the Convention's drafters deliberately separated. William Schabas, in his treatise, has long emphasized that "genocide is the crime of crimes" precisely because of dolus specialis and has warned against expansive readings that dissolve the category (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2009; Schabas, Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025).

Second, the ICJ standard of proof is "fully conclusive." In *Bosnia v. Serbia* (2007) and *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015), the ICJ held that genocidal intent against a State must be "fully conclusive" and that where conduct is "capable of two reasonable inferences," the inference of genocidal intent cannot be drawn. The Court has only once before this case found a state act of genocide (Srebrenica). Geoffrey Nice KC, the former Milosevic prosecutor, has publicly noted the unusually demanding ICJ standard while saying that statements by Israeli leaders and the pattern of conduct have brought the case within plausibility (Nice, *Express Tribune*, "Unpacking the ICJ ruling," 2024).

## 4. Scholars rejecting or qualifying the framing

The scholarly field has shifted considerably between October 2023 and May 2026. The strongest version of the counter-position requires distinguishing three categories.

**(a) Scholars rejecting the characterization outright.** Eugene Kontorovich (George Mason Antonin Scalia School of Law) and Avi Bell (Bar-Ilan / University of San Diego) have argued throughout that the conduct does not meet the legal definition. Kontorovich: "This isn't genocide. It's a state acting to prevent another genocide, the one Hamas proudly seeks" (Kontorovich, "Israel can limit the ICJ's potential damage," *JNS*, January 29, 2024; Bell co-authored critique, *University of Minnesota Journal of International Law*, 2024). Their argument rests on the dolus specialis threshold, the Article 51 framing, and the shielding doctrine.

**(b) Scholars qualifying (serious violations occurred; specific-intent threshold not met or not yet met).** Yuval Shany and Amichai Cohen of the Israel Democracy Institute and Hebrew University have argued that grave IHL violations have likely occurred but the specific-intent test for genocide is a categorically different question that the public record does not yet establish to the ICJ standard (Shany and Cohen, Lieber Institute West Point Symposium, 2023; Cohen interview, *Times of Israel*, "Is the IDF acting legally in Gaza?"; Lawfare and Just Security pieces, 2024–2025). Tom Dannenbaum (Tufts/Fletcher) and Janina Dill (Oxford) published a closely cited analytical piece in the *American Journal of International Law* examining belligerent intent across three legal functions, concluding the ICJ provisional measures order is best read narrowly on the plausibility question; the piece does not endorse the genocide characterization on the merits (Dannenbaum and Dill, "International Law in Gaza: Belligerent Intent and Provisional Measures," *AJIL* 118:4, 2024). Kevin Jon Heller (University of Copenhagen) has criticized loose inferences of dolus specialis from political rhetoric while accepting that the conduct elements of Article II have substantial support; his position is qualified in that he treats the intent question as legally distinct and methodologically demanding (Heller commentary in Dannenbaum/Dill 2024; Opinio Juris and EJIL: Talk!, 2024).

**(c) Scholars who have evolved.** William Schabas had historically warned against overuse of the genocide label and emphasized the high dolus specialis bar. Over 2024–2025 Schabas publicly characterized *South Africa v. Israel* as "arguably the strongest case of genocide that has ever come before the Court," citing the Gallant statement, the pattern of conduct, and the destruction of conditions of life (Schabas, ECPS interview, "US, Germany, and Others Could Be Held Liable as Accomplices"; Anadolu Agency, "South Africa's genocide case against Israel strongest ever at ICJ," 2025; Schabas, Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025). The evolution complicates use of Schabas's earlier work in support of the counter-position.

Omer Bartov (Brown University; Israeli-born Holocaust and genocide historian) is the most documented evolution. In a November 2023 *New York Times* op-ed, Bartov concluded that war crimes and crimes against humanity were likely but the genocide threshold had not been met. By a July 2025 *New York Times* op-ed, he wrote that conduct had crossed into genocide, citing the May 2024 Rafah operation and policy implementation of earlier statements (NPR, "Historian Omer Bartov on why he believes Israel is committing genocide in Gaza," July 17, 2025; Bartov, "Israel's war in Gaza and the question of genocide," *Journal of Genocide Research*, 2025).

Philippe Sands KC has occupied a nuanced public position. Sands represented Palestine in the parallel ICJ advisory proceedings on the legality of the occupation in February 2024 but, in commentary on the Convention, has emphasized the gap between popular and legal usage of "genocide," warning that "into that gap a lot of mischief is made" (Sands, *New York Times* interview; Chatham House podcast with Bronwen Maddox, 2024). His public position has trended toward acceptance of the characterization over time while insisting on legal rigor about specific intent.

Geoffrey Nice KC has cautioned that the ICJ "fully conclusive" standard is unusually demanding while saying the statements and pattern of conduct put the case within plausibility (Nice, *Express Tribune*, 2024).

## 5. October 7 as legal and factual context

The Hamas-led assault on October 7, 2023 killed approximately 1,200 people in Israel, the substantial majority civilians, including 36 children; approximately 5,400 were wounded; approximately 251 persons were taken hostage to Gaza (Human Rights Watch, "October 7 Crimes Against Humanity, War Crimes by Hamas-led Groups," July 17, 2024; Amnesty International, "Targeting Civilians," 2024).

The Patten report (UN SRSG on Sexual Violence in Conflict, March 4, 2024) found "reasonable grounds to believe that conflict-related sexual violence, including rape and gang-rape, occurred across multiple locations of Israel and the Gaza periphery during the attacks on 7 October 2023" and "clear and convincing information" of sexual violence including rape and sexualized torture against hostages held in Gaza (Patten, Mission Report, UN, March 2024). The report is a fact-finding mission and explicitly notes its limits (not a criminal investigation). The UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry (Pillay et al.) documented extensive war crimes and crimes against humanity in the conduct of Hamas and allied groups on October 7.

The 1988 Hamas Covenant contained explicitly antisemitic language and called for the destruction of Israel. The 2017 "Document of General Principles and Policies" replaced the religious anti-Jewish framing with a nationalist anti-"Zionist project" framing while continuing to refuse recognition of Israel and asserting claims "from the river to the sea" (Hamas, "A Document of General Principles and Policies," 1 May 2017; Institute for Palestine Studies, "A Newer Hamas?"). Israel and several scholars argue the 2017 document is rhetorical rebranding and that operational policy and post-October 7 statements remain destructive of Israel's existence; others argue the document reflects a substantive narrowing.

Senior Hamas figures have publicly affirmed the intent to repeat October 7. Khaled Mashal, in an October 2024 Reuters interview, framed October 7 as a "pivotal moment" and affirmed the cyclical character of Palestinian resistance (MEMRI translation of Mashal statements, 2024; *Arab Weekly*). Earlier interviews by Ghazi Hamad and others stated October 7 would be repeated "again and again." Some scholars argue that statements of intent by a non-state armed group to destroy a protected group raise Article II analysis against Hamas itself, independent of how the question runs against Israel (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2009; Bell and Kontorovich commentary on the bilateral character of Article II analysis, 2024).

## 6. Hamas use of civilian infrastructure: documented record

The record is partial, contested in places, and verified in others. Cataloguing it accurately is necessary to the counter-position because Israel's IHL argument depends on it.

**Tunnels generally.** Hamas constructed an extensive tunnel network in Gaza, much running beneath civilian areas. UN OCHA briefings, IDF documentation, and third-party reporting confirm the existence and scale (IDF Spokesperson briefings; *New York Times* tunnel-mapping investigations, 2024).

**Al-Shifa Hospital.** Israel claimed Al-Shifa sat above a Hamas command-and-control complex; the IDF released video and stills of weapons, a tunnel shaft, and an underground corridor. A *Washington Post* analysis in December 2023 concluded that publicly released material showed "no immediate evidence of military use by Hamas" in the specific rooms the IDF had highlighted as connected to the tunnel network (*Washington Post*, "Investigating the assault on al-Shifa," December 21, 2023). A *New York Times* report in February 2024, based on classified Israeli documents, reported weapons storage and tunnels at the site while stating that the publicly produced evidence did not establish a full command-and-control center (*New York Times*, February 2024). The IDF's December 2024 comments to the OHCHR Fourth Thematic Report on hospitals defended the operation and disputed OHCHR's framing (Israel comments to OHCHR, December 2024, UN unispal archive).

**UNRWA installations.** In February 2024, the IDF reported a 700-meter, 18-meter-deep tunnel network beneath UNRWA's Gaza City headquarters, including a Hamas data center; UNRWA Commissioner-General Lazzarini stated UNRWA had vacated the building on October 12, 2023 and had not known of the underground infrastructure (Al Jazeera, "UNRWA refutes Israeli implications," February 11, 2024; FDD, "Hamas Data Center Found Under UNRWA Headquarters," February 12, 2024). The Colonna Report (April 20, 2024) concluded that UNRWA had a more developed neutrality regime than comparable UN agencies but recommended substantial reforms; UNRWA dismissed several staff alleged to have participated in October 7 (Colonna et al., April 2024). The *New York Times* in 2024 reported documents indicating that senior UNRWA school administrators were Hamas members (*Times of Israel* citing *NYT*, 2024).

**Schools and residential buildings.** IDF documentation includes photographs and internal Hamas documents indicating weapons storage in UNRWA and other schools. Specific Israeli strike justifications have been disputed in some cases by UN agencies and journalists. Hamas has acknowledged operations from residential areas in some statements while disputing specific Israeli site identifications.

The honest record is that Hamas's use of civilian infrastructure is established as a general pattern, while specific Israeli identifications of particular sites have been verified in some cases, partially verified in others, and disputed in still others. The counter-position relies on the general pattern; the Court will assess particular instances.

## 7. Israeli internal pluralism

"Israel's position" is not monolithic. Israeli human rights organizations have produced some of the most rigorous documentation of conduct in Gaza and the West Bank, and several have adopted positions sharply at odds with the government's.

B'Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights – Israel jointly published "Our Genocide" on July 28, 2025, an 88-page report concluding that "since the Hamas attack on 7 October 2023, Israel has acted in a coordinated and deliberate manner to destroy Palestinian society in the Gaza Strip, committing genocide against its residents" (B'Tselem and PHRI, *Our Genocide*, July 28, 2025; B'Tselem press release, July 28, 2025; CNN, "For first time, two leading Israeli human rights groups accuse Israel of genocide in Gaza," July 28, 2025). The report invokes the legal framework of the Convention. It was the first time either organization adopted the legal label.

Adalah, the Legal Center for Arab Minority Rights in Israel, in a letter dated April 8, 2024 to Israel's Attorney General, State Attorney, Police Commissioner and Minister of Justice, demanded criminal investigation of incitement to genocide by Israeli public figures, citing the Israeli Crime of Genocide (Prevention and Punishment) Law, 5710-1950 and the ICJ provisional measures order (Adalah, "Adalah Demands Israel Investigate and Prosecute Incitement to Genocide by Public Figures," April 8, 2024).

Yesh Din, with eleven other Israeli human rights organizations, characterized 2025 as the deadliest and most destructive year for Palestinians since 1967 and has documented West Bank settler violence and IDF conduct in detail. Its public position has stopped short of the genocide label while using "ethnic cleansing" and "crimes against humanity" framing in joint statements.

The Association for Civil Rights in Israel (ACRI) has been more cautious about the genocide framing but has joined petitions to the Israeli Supreme Court on humanitarian access, detention conditions, and freedom of expression for war critics.

A counter-position section that flattens "Israel" into "the Israeli government" misrepresents the empirical record. The strongest version of the Israeli government's legal argument exists in tension with assessments of significant parts of Israeli civil society.
