# Counter-Position: The Case Against the Genocide Characterization

*Sub-Agent 7 input to* Application of the Genocide Convention to the Israel–Palestine Conflict: An Evidentiary Review (as of May 2026)*. This catalogue records the case against the genocide characterization at its strongest. It records arguments as arguments; the ICJ will adjudicate.*

## 1. Israel's legal argument at the ICJ

Israel's defense has unfolded in three filings: oral argument at the provisional measures phase on 12 January 2024; written observations on subsequent South African requests; and the counter-memorial filed 12 March 2026, after two extensions of the original July 2025 deadline (ICJ Case 192 docket; Anadolu Agency, 2025). Pleadings remain largely confidential pending oral hearings; the public structure of the argument is consistent across the January 2024 oral round, MFA statements, and the March 2026 filing notice.

**(a) War aim is destruction of Hamas, not of Palestinians.** Opening for Israel on January 12, 2024, MFA Legal Adviser Tal Becker framed the operation as self-defense against an armed group whose stated objective was Israel's destruction and called South Africa's case "a libel" intended to "deny Israel the right to defend itself" (Becker, CR 2024/2, 12 January 2024). On the counter-memorial, the Israeli MFA said Israel "has submitted its Counter-Memorial to the ICJ, rejecting South Africa's fabricated and politicized blood libel" and that "the court lacks jurisdiction, and South Africa's allegations should be dismissed in their entirety" (Israel MFA, 14 March 2026; *Daily Maverick*, 16 March 2026).

**(b) UN Charter Article 51 self-defense.** Israel argues the operation is a lawful response to the October 7 armed attack and is exercised under Article 51 for hostage recovery (Becker, CR 2024/2; Israel MFA releases). The Court did not rule on Article 51 at the provisional measures stage.

**(c) Dual-use targeting under IHL distinction.** Galit Raguan and Christopher Staker argued that Hamas's embedding inside hospitals, schools, mosques, and residential buildings converts those sites to lawful military objectives once used to make an effective contribution to military action. Staker: "The inevitable fatalities and human suffering of any conflict is not of itself a pattern of conduct that plausibly shows genocidal intent" (Staker, CR 2024/2; Al Jazeera, "ICJ genocide case: What are Israel's arguments?", 12 January 2024).

**(d) Evacuation warnings, corridors, aid.** Raguan walked the Court through IDF evacuation notices, leaflet drops, telephone warnings, and humanitarian corridors as positive evidence that intent was not to destroy the group as such (Raguan, CR 2024/2; IDF Spokesperson statements). South Africa, OCHA, and others contest operational adequacy; Israel cites their existence as inconsistent with Article II specific intent.

**(e) Treatment of officials' statements.** Israel argued that statements South Africa relies on (Defense Minister Gallant's October 9, 2023 "no electricity, no food, no water, no fuel" remark; PM Netanyahu's "Amalek" reference; comments by Minister Smotrich and others) are either (i) rhetorical and non-operational, (ii) made by figures outside the military chain of command, (iii) extracted from longer statements in which Israel reaffirmed IHL compliance, or (iv) contradicted by Cabinet decisions and IDF rules of engagement (Becker and Noam, CR 2024/2; *Haaretz*, "Israel Pushes Back at ICJ Gaza Genocide Charge," 11 January 2024). Israel invokes *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015) for the proposition that statements must be evaluated against actual state conduct.

**(f) Hamas shielding doctrine.** Civilian harm caused by an attacker against a target shielded by the defending force does not transfer mens rea to the attacker for genocide purposes, and documented Hamas use of civilian infrastructure shifts material responsibility (Noam, CR 2024/2).

**(g) Plausibility as procedural, not merits.** Israel reads the January 26, 2024 order as finding only that some rights asserted by South Africa were "plausible" for jurisdictional purposes, and notes that the Court expressly did not rule on whether genocide is occurring (ICJ Order of 26 January 2024, paras. 30, 54, 74; Israel MFA, 26 January 2024). Scholars critical of Israel's broader position have agreed with this narrow reading (Heller, Opinio Juris; Akande and Milanovic, EJIL: Talk!, January–February 2024).

**(h) Domestic legal oversight.** Israel cites the Military Advocate General, the Supreme Court, and the Attorney General as functioning oversight institutions. The record is contested. The IDF MAG opened investigations into Sde Teiman detainee abuse (CNN, "Major General Yifat Tomer Yerushalmi," 4 November 2025), but the MAG resigned in late 2025 amid politicized backlash, and on March 12, 2026 her successor withdrew the criminal charges against five reservists (Israel Democracy Institute, "End of the Sde Teiman Abuse Case," 2026). Critics argue the sequence undercuts the oversight argument; Israel argues the existence of investigation, prosecution, and judicial review is itself probative of non-genocidal intent.

## 2. The IHL framework Israel invokes

Israel's substantive legal position is that civilian harm in dense urban combat against an armed group embedded in civilian infrastructure is governed by IHL (distinction, proportionality, precautions) and not by the Convention's specific-intent test. The argument has two prongs.

First, the legal categories are distinct. War crimes punish unlawful conduct in hostilities. Crimes against humanity punish widespread or systematic attack on civilians. Genocide requires the additional narrow mens rea of intent to destroy a protected group as such. Conflating IHL violations with genocide, on this view, collapses categories the Convention's drafters deliberately separated. William Schabas has long emphasized that "genocide is the crime of crimes" precisely because of dolus specialis and has warned against expansive readings that dissolve the category (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009).

Second, the ICJ standard of proof is "fully conclusive." In *Bosnia v. Serbia* (2007) and *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015) the Court held that where conduct is "capable of two reasonable inferences," the inference of genocidal intent cannot be drawn. The Court has only once before this case found a state act of genocide (Srebrenica). Geoffrey Nice KC, the former Milosevic prosecutor, has noted the unusually demanding standard while saying that the statements and pattern of conduct have brought the present case within plausibility (Nice, *Express Tribune*, 2024).

## 4. Scholars rejecting or qualifying the framing

The scholarly field has shifted considerably between October 2023 and May 2026. The strongest version of the counter-position requires distinguishing three categories.

**(a) Outright rejection.** Eugene Kontorovich (George Mason Antonin Scalia) and Avi Bell (Bar-Ilan / San Diego) argue the conduct does not meet the legal definition. Kontorovich: "This isn't genocide. It's a state acting to prevent another genocide, the one Hamas proudly seeks" (*JNS*, 29 January 2024; Bell, *University of Minnesota Journal of International Law*, 2024). The argument rests on dolus specialis, the Article 51 framing, and the shielding doctrine.

**(b) Qualified (serious violations occurred; specific-intent threshold not met or not yet met).** Yuval Shany and Amichai Cohen (Israel Democracy Institute; Hebrew University) argue grave IHL violations have likely occurred but that the specific-intent test is a categorically different question that the public record does not yet establish to the ICJ standard (Shany and Cohen, Lieber Institute symposium, 2023; Cohen, *Times of Israel*, "Is the IDF acting legally in Gaza?"; Lawfare and Just Security, 2024–2025). Tom Dannenbaum (Tufts/Fletcher) and Janina Dill (Oxford), in the *American Journal of International Law*, examine belligerent intent across three legal functions and read the ICJ provisional measures order narrowly on plausibility; the piece does not endorse the genocide characterization on the merits ("International Law in Gaza: Belligerent Intent and Provisional Measures," *AJIL* 118:4, 2024). Kevin Jon Heller (Copenhagen) has criticized loose inferences of dolus specialis from political rhetoric while accepting that the conduct elements of Article II have substantial support; he treats the intent question as legally distinct and methodologically demanding (Heller in Dannenbaum/Dill 2024; Opinio Juris and EJIL: Talk!, 2024).

**(c) Evolved positions.** William Schabas had historically warned against overuse of the label and emphasized the high dolus specialis bar. Over 2024–2025 Schabas publicly called *South Africa v. Israel* "arguably the strongest case of genocide that has ever come before the Court," citing the Gallant statement, the pattern of conduct, and the destruction of conditions of life (Schabas, ECPS interview; Anadolu Agency, 2025; Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025). The evolution complicates use of his earlier work in support of the counter-position.

Omer Bartov (Brown; Israeli-born Holocaust and genocide historian) is the most documented evolution. In a November 2023 *New York Times* op-ed he concluded war crimes and crimes against humanity were likely but the genocide threshold had not been met. By a July 2025 *NYT* op-ed he wrote that conduct had crossed into genocide, citing the May 2024 Rafah operation and implementation of earlier statements (NPR interview, 17 July 2025; Bartov, *Journal of Genocide Research*, 2025).

Philippe Sands KC has been nuanced. He represented Palestine in the parallel ICJ advisory proceedings in February 2024, but in commentary on the Convention has emphasized the gap between popular and legal usage of "genocide," warning that "into that gap a lot of mischief is made" (Sands, *New York Times* interview; Chatham House podcast, 2024). His public position has trended toward acceptance of the characterization while insisting on legal rigor about specific intent.

Geoffrey Nice KC has cautioned that the "fully conclusive" standard is unusually demanding while saying the statements and pattern of conduct put the case within plausibility (Nice, *Express Tribune*, 2024).

## 5. October 7 as legal and factual context

The Hamas-led assault on October 7, 2023 killed approximately 1,200 people in Israel, the substantial majority civilians (including 36 children); approximately 5,400 were wounded; approximately 251 persons were taken hostage to Gaza (HRW, "October 7 Crimes Against Humanity, War Crimes by Hamas-led Groups," 17 July 2024; Amnesty International, "Targeting Civilians," 2024).

The Patten report (UN SRSG on Sexual Violence in Conflict, 4 March 2024) found "reasonable grounds to believe that conflict-related sexual violence, including rape and gang-rape, occurred across multiple locations of Israel and the Gaza periphery during the attacks on 7 October 2023" and "clear and convincing information" of sexual violence including rape and sexualized torture against hostages held in Gaza (Patten, Mission Report, UN, March 2024). The report is a fact-finding mission and explicitly notes its limits (not a criminal investigation). The UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry (Pillay et al.) documented extensive war crimes and crimes against humanity by Hamas and allied groups on October 7.

The 1988 Hamas Covenant contained explicitly antisemitic language and called for Israel's destruction. The 2017 "Document of General Principles and Policies" replaced the religious anti-Jewish framing with a nationalist anti-"Zionist project" framing while continuing to refuse recognition of Israel and asserting claims "from the river to the sea" (Hamas, 1 May 2017; Institute for Palestine Studies, "A Newer Hamas?"). Israel and several scholars argue the 2017 document is rhetorical rebranding and that operational policy and post-October 7 statements remain destructive of Israel's existence; others argue the document reflects substantive narrowing.

Senior Hamas figures have publicly affirmed the intent to repeat October 7. Khaled Mashal, in an October 2024 Reuters interview, framed October 7 as a "pivotal moment" and affirmed the cyclical character of Palestinian resistance (MEMRI translation, 2024; *Arab Weekly*). Earlier interviews by Ghazi Hamad and others stated October 7 would be repeated "again and again." Scholars including Bell and Kontorovich argue that such statements implicate Article II analysis against Hamas itself, independent of how the question runs against Israel (Bell and Kontorovich commentary, 2024).

## 6. Hamas use of civilian infrastructure: documented record

The record is partial, contested in places, and verified in others. Cataloguing it accurately is necessary because Israel's IHL argument depends on it.

**Tunnels.** Hamas constructed an extensive tunnel network in Gaza, much beneath civilian areas. UN OCHA briefings, IDF documentation, and third-party reporting confirm the existence and scale (IDF Spokesperson briefings; *New York Times* tunnel-mapping investigations, 2024).

**Al-Shifa Hospital.** Israel claimed Al-Shifa sat above a Hamas command-and-control complex; the IDF released video and stills of weapons, a tunnel shaft, and an underground corridor. A *Washington Post* analysis in December 2023 concluded that publicly released material showed "no immediate evidence of military use by Hamas" in the specific rooms the IDF had highlighted as connected to the tunnel network (*Washington Post*, "Investigating the assault on al-Shifa," 21 December 2023). A *New York Times* report in February 2024, based on classified Israeli documents, reported weapons storage and tunnels at the site while stating that the publicly produced evidence did not establish a full command-and-control center (*New York Times*, February 2024). The IDF's December 2024 comments to the OHCHR Fourth Thematic Report defended the operation and disputed OHCHR's framing (Israel comments, UN unispal archive).

**UNRWA installations.** In February 2024 the IDF reported a 700-meter, 18-meter-deep tunnel network beneath UNRWA's Gaza City headquarters, including a Hamas data center; UNRWA Commissioner-General Lazzarini said UNRWA had vacated the building on 12 October 2023 and had not known of the underground infrastructure (Al Jazeera, 11 February 2024; FDD, 12 February 2024). The Colonna Report (20 April 2024) concluded UNRWA had a more developed neutrality regime than comparable UN agencies but recommended substantial reforms; UNRWA dismissed several staff alleged to have participated in October 7. The *New York Times* in 2024 reported documents indicating senior UNRWA school administrators were Hamas members.

**Schools and residential buildings.** IDF documentation includes photographs and internal Hamas documents indicating weapons storage in UNRWA and other schools. Specific Israeli strike justifications have been disputed in some cases by UN agencies and journalists. Hamas has acknowledged operations from residential areas in some statements while disputing specific Israeli site identifications.

The honest record: Hamas's use of civilian infrastructure is established as a general pattern, while specific Israeli identifications of particular sites have been verified in some cases, partially verified in others, and disputed in still others. The counter-position relies on the general pattern; the Court will assess particular instances.

## 7. Israeli internal pluralism

"Israel's position" is not monolithic. Israeli human rights organizations have produced some of the most rigorous documentation of conduct in Gaza and the West Bank, and several have adopted positions sharply at odds with the government's.

B'Tselem and Physicians for Human Rights – Israel jointly published *Our Genocide* on July 28, 2025, an 88-page report concluding that "since the Hamas attack on 7 October 2023, Israel has acted in a coordinated and deliberate manner to destroy Palestinian society in the Gaza Strip, committing genocide against its residents" (B'Tselem and PHRI, *Our Genocide*, 28 July 2025; CNN, "For first time, two leading Israeli human rights groups accuse Israel of genocide in Gaza," 28 July 2025). The report invokes the Convention's legal framework. It was the first time either organization adopted the legal label.

Adalah, the Legal Center for Arab Minority Rights in Israel, in a letter dated 8 April 2024 to Israel's Attorney General, State Attorney, Police Commissioner and Minister of Justice, demanded criminal investigation of incitement to genocide by Israeli public figures, citing the Israeli Crime of Genocide (Prevention and Punishment) Law, 5710-1950 and the ICJ provisional measures order (Adalah, "Adalah Demands Israel Investigate and Prosecute Incitement to Genocide by Public Figures," 8 April 2024).

Yesh Din, with eleven other Israeli human rights organizations, characterized 2025 as the deadliest and most destructive year for Palestinians since 1967 and has documented West Bank settler violence and IDF conduct in detail. Its public position has stopped short of the genocide label while using "ethnic cleansing" and "crimes against humanity" framing in joint statements. ACRI has been more cautious about the genocide framing but has joined petitions to the Supreme Court on humanitarian access, detention conditions, and freedom of expression for war critics.

A counter-position section that flattens "Israel" into "the Israeli government" misrepresents the empirical record. The strongest version of the Israeli government's legal argument exists in tension with assessments of significant parts of Israeli civil society.
