# Counter-Position: The Case Against the Genocide Characterization

*Sub-Agent 7 input to* Application of the Genocide Convention to the Israel–Palestine Conflict: An Evidentiary Review (as of May 2026)*. This catalogue records the legal and factual case against the genocide characterization at its strongest. It does not endorse that case. It records arguments as arguments. The ICJ will adjudicate the merits.*

## 1. Israel's legal argument at the ICJ

Israel's defense at the ICJ has unfolded in three filings to date: oral argument at the provisional measures phase on January 12, 2024; further written observations on subsequent South African requests; and the counter-memorial filed March 12, 2026, after two extensions of the original July 2025 deadline (ICJ, "Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide in the Gaza Strip (South Africa v. Israel)," Case 192 docket; "ICJ grants Israel another extension," Anadolu Agency, 2025). Israel's pleadings have remained largely confidential pending oral hearings, but the public structure of its argument is consistent across the January 2024 oral round, MFA statements, and the March 2026 filing notice.

**(a) The war aim is defined as destruction of Hamas, not of Palestinians.** In opening for Israel on January 12, 2024, MFA Legal Adviser Tal Becker framed the operation as one of self-defense against an armed group whose stated objective was the destruction of Israel, and characterized South Africa's case as "a libel" intended to "deny Israel the right to defend itself" (Becker, oral statement, ICJ Verbatim Record CR 2024/2, 12 January 2024). On the counter-memorial, the Israeli MFA stated that Israel "has submitted its Counter-Memorial to the ICJ, rejecting South Africa's fabricated and politicized blood libel" and that "the court lacks jurisdiction, and South Africa's allegations should be dismissed in their entirety" (Israel MFA statement, March 14, 2026, reported in Daily Maverick, "Don't hold your breath," March 16, 2026).

**(b) UN Charter Article 51 self-defense.** Israel has argued throughout that the operation is a lawful response to the October 7 armed attack by Hamas and allied groups, exercised under Article 51 of the UN Charter and consistent with continuing hostage-recovery objectives (Becker, CR 2024/2; Israel MFA, "Hamas-Israel conflict 2023" releases). The ICJ has not ruled on the Article 51 argument in this case; the Court's January 26, 2024 provisional measures order addressed only plausibility under the Genocide Convention.

**(c) Dual-use targeting under IHL distinction analysis.** Israel's counsel Galit Raguan and Christopher Staker presented an IHL framework arguing that Hamas's embedding inside hospitals, schools, mosques, and residential buildings converts those sites to lawful military objectives under the principle of distinction once they are used to make an effective contribution to military action. Staker argued: "The inevitable fatalities and human suffering of any conflict is not of itself a pattern of conduct that plausibly shows genocidal intent" (Staker, CR 2024/2; reporting in Al Jazeera, "ICJ genocide case: What are Israel's arguments and do they hold up?," January 12, 2024).

**(d) Evacuation warnings, humanitarian corridors, and aid as evidence of non-genocidal intent.** Raguan walked the Court through IDF-issued evacuation notices, leaflet drops, telephone warnings, and the opening of humanitarian corridors as positive evidence that Israel's intent was not to destroy the Palestinian group as such (Raguan, CR 2024/2; IDF Spokesperson statements, October 2023 through 2024). South Africa, UN OCHA, and others contest the operational adequacy of these measures; Israel cites their existence as inconsistent with the specific intent required by Article II.

**(e) Treatment of officials' statements.** Israel's counsel argued that statements South Africa relies on (e.g., Defense Minister Gallant's October 9, 2023 "no electricity, no food, no water, no fuel" remark; Prime Minister Netanyahu's "Amalek" reference; comments by Minister Smotrich and others) are either (i) rhetorical and not operational, (ii) made by figures outside the military chain of command, (iii) extracted from longer statements in which Israel reaffirmed compliance with international law, or (iv) contradicted by formal Cabinet decisions and binding IDF rules of engagement (Becker and Noam, CR 2024/2; reporting in Haaretz, "Israel Pushes Back at ICJ Gaza Genocide Charge, Urges Court to Scrutinize Gov't Decisions, Not 'Random' Statements," January 11, 2024). On the legal point, Israel invokes the ICJ's *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015) holding that statements must be evaluated for their relationship to actual state conduct.

**(f) Hamas shielding doctrine.** Israel argues that under IHL, civilian harm caused by an attacker against a target shielded by the defending force does not transfer mens rea to the attacker for genocide purposes, and that Hamas's documented use of hospitals, schools, and residential buildings shifts a material portion of civilian harm to Hamas's own conduct (Noam, CR 2024/2; IDF Spokesperson briefings on Al-Shifa, Indonesia Hospital, and UNRWA installations).

**(g) The plausibility finding is procedural, not a merits judgment.** Israel has been consistent that the January 26, 2024 provisional measures order found only that some of the rights asserted by South Africa were "plausible" in the sense required for jurisdiction over provisional measures, and that the Court expressly did not rule on whether genocide is or is not occurring. Israel cites Judge Bhandari's separate opinion and the ICJ's own framing (ICJ Order of 26 January 2024, paragraphs 30, 54, 74; Israel MFA response statement, January 26, 2024). Several scholars critical of Israel's broader position have agreed with this reading of the procedural posture (Heller, Opinio Juris commentary; Akande and Milanovic, EJIL: Talk!, January–February 2024).

**(h) Domestic legal oversight.** Israel cites the Military Advocate General fact-finding mechanism, the Israeli Supreme Court sitting as the High Court of Justice, and the Attorney General's office as functioning institutions of internal accountability. The record on this is contested. The IDF MAG opened investigations into Sde Teiman detainee abuse and other incidents (CNN, "Major General Yifat Tomer Yerushalmi," November 4, 2025), but the MAG resigned in late 2025 amid a politicized backlash, and on March 12, 2026 her successor announced the withdrawal of criminal charges against five reservists in the Sde Teiman case (Israel Democracy Institute, "End of the Sde Teiman Abuse Case: The IDF MAG Withdraws Indictments," 2026). Critics argue this sequence undercuts the credibility of the domestic-oversight argument; Israel argues that the existence of investigation, prosecution, and judicial review is itself probative of non-genocidal state intent.

## 2. The IHL framework Israel invokes

Israel's substantive legal position rests on the proposition that civilian harm in dense urban combat against an armed group embedded in civilian infrastructure is governed by the *jus in bello* of IHL (distinction, proportionality, precautions in attack) and not by the Genocide Convention's specific-intent test. The argument has two prongs.

First, that the legal categories are distinct. War crimes punish unlawful conduct in hostilities. Crimes against humanity punish widespread or systematic attack on civilians. Genocide requires the additional, narrow mens rea of intent to destroy a protected group as such. Conflating IHL violations with genocide, on this view, collapses categories the Convention's drafters deliberately separated. William Schabas, in his treatise and earlier work, repeatedly emphasized that "genocide is the crime of crimes" precisely because of dolus specialis, and warned against expansive readings that would dissolve the category (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., Cambridge University Press, 2009; Schabas, "Genocide: The Crime of Crimes," Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025, where he reiterates the conceptual importance of the specific-intent requirement even as he applies it to Gaza).

Second, that the ICJ standard of proof is "fully conclusive." In *Bosnia v. Serbia* (2007) and *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015), the ICJ held that proof of genocidal intent against a State must be "fully conclusive" and that where conduct is "capable of two reasonable inferences," the inference of genocidal intent cannot be drawn. Israel argues this is a deliberate high bar; the Court has only once before this case found a state act of genocide established (Srebrenica). Geoffrey Nice KC, the former Milosevic prosecutor, has publicly noted both the unusually high ICJ standard and that, in his own assessment, statements by Israeli leaders combined with the pattern of conduct have nonetheless brought the case within plausibility (Nice, public commentary cited in Express Tribune, "Unpacking the ICJ ruling," 2024). Israel relies on the standard; opponents argue the inference of intent is now reasonable.

## 4. Scholars rejecting or qualifying the framing

The scholarly field has shifted considerably between October 2023 and May 2026. The strongest version of the counter-position requires distinguishing three categories.

**(a) Scholars rejecting the characterization outright.** Eugene Kontorovich (George Mason Antonin Scalia School of Law) and Avi Bell (Bar-Ilan / University of San Diego) have argued throughout that the conduct does not meet the legal definition; Kontorovich described the ICJ proceedings as "a judicial Oct. 7" and stated, "This isn't genocide. It's a state acting to prevent another genocide, the one Hamas proudly seeks" (Kontorovich, "Israel can limit the ICJ's potential damage," *JNS*, January 29, 2024; Bell co-authored critique in University of Minnesota Journal of International Law, 2024). Their argument rests on (i) the dolus specialis threshold, (ii) the Article 51 framing, and (iii) the Hamas-shielding doctrine.

**(b) Scholars qualifying (serious violations occurred; specific-intent threshold not met or not yet met).** Yuval Shany and Amichai Cohen of the Israel Democracy Institute and Hebrew University have published extensively on Israel's conduct, generally arguing that grave IHL violations have likely occurred but that the specific-intent test for genocide is a categorically different question that the public record does not yet establish to the ICJ standard (Shany and Cohen, Lieber Institute West Point Symposium, "International Law 'Made in Israel'," 2023; Cohen interview in *Times of Israel*, "What Matters Now to Prof. Amichai Cohen: Is the IDF acting legally in Gaza?"; ongoing Lawfare and Just Security pieces, 2024–2025). Tom Dannenbaum (Tufts/Fletcher) and Janina Dill (Oxford) wrote a closely cited analytical piece in the American Journal of International Law that examines belligerent intent across three legal functions and concludes the ICJ provisional measures order is best read narrowly on the plausibility question; the piece does not endorse the genocide characterization on the merits (Dannenbaum and Dill, "International Law in Gaza: Belligerent Intent and Provisional Measures," *AJIL* 118:4, 2024). Kevin Jon Heller (University of Copenhagen) has, in successive commentaries, criticized loose inferences of dolus specialis from political rhetoric while accepting that the conduct elements of Article II have substantial support; his position is "qualified" in the sense that he treats the intent question as legally distinct and methodologically demanding (Heller, contributions cited in Dannenbaum/Dill 2024; Opinio Juris and EJIL: Talk! commentary, 2024).

**(c) Scholars who have evolved.** William Schabas (Middlesex University; longtime author on the Convention) had historically warned against overuse of the genocide label and emphasized the high dolus specialis bar. Over 2024–2025, Schabas publicly characterized *South Africa v. Israel* as "arguably the strongest case of genocide that has ever come before the Court," citing the Gallant statement, the pattern of conduct, and the destruction of conditions of life (Schabas, ECPS interview, "US, Germany, and Others Could Be Held Liable as Accomplices"; Anadolu Agency, "South Africa's genocide case against Israel strongest ever at ICJ," 2025; Schabas, Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025). His evolution is significant because it complicates the use of his earlier work to support the counter-position.

Omer Bartov (Brown University; Israeli-born Holocaust and genocide historian) is the most documented evolution. In a November 2023 *New York Times* op-ed, Bartov concluded that war crimes and crimes against humanity were likely but the genocide threshold had not been met. By a July 2025 *New York Times* op-ed, he wrote that Israel's conduct had crossed into genocide, citing the May 2024 Rafah operation and statements implemented through policy (NPR interview, "Historian Omer Bartov on why he believes Israel is committing genocide in Gaza," July 17, 2025; Bartov, "Israel's war in Gaza and the question of genocide," *Journal of Genocide Research*, 2025).

Philippe Sands KC has occupied a nuanced public position. Sands represented Palestine in the parallel ICJ advisory proceedings on the legality of the occupation in February 2024, but in commentary on the Genocide Convention has emphasized the gap between popular and legal usage of "genocide," warning that "into that gap a lot of mischief is made" (Sands, *New York Times* interview; Chatham House podcast with Bronwen Maddox, 2024). His public position has trended toward acceptance of the characterization over time but he has consistently insisted on legal rigor about the specific-intent test.

Geoffrey Nice KC has cautioned that the ICJ "fully conclusive" standard is unusually demanding and that historically states evade timely recognition of genocide; he has nonetheless said the statements and pattern of conduct put the case within plausibility (Nice, *Express Tribune* commentary, 2024).

## 5. October 7 as legal and factual context

The Hamas-led assault on October 7, 2023 killed approximately 1,200 people in Israel, the substantial majority civilians, including 36 children; approximately 5,400 were wounded; approximately 251 persons were taken hostage to Gaza (Human Rights Watch, "October 7 Crimes Against Humanity, War Crimes by Hamas-led Groups," July 17, 2024; Amnesty International, "Targeting Civilians," 2024).

The Patten report (UN Special Representative of the Secretary-General on Sexual Violence in Conflict, March 4, 2024) found "reasonable grounds to believe that conflict-related sexual violence, including rape and gang-rape, occurred across multiple locations of Israel and the Gaza periphery during the attacks on 7 October 2023" and "clear and convincing information" of sexual violence including rape and sexualized torture against hostages held in Gaza (Patten, Report on official visit to Israel and the OPT, UN, March 2024; UN Security Council briefing, "Reasonable Grounds to Believe Conflict-Related Sexual Violence Occurred"). The report is a fact-finding mission and explicitly notes its limits (not a criminal investigation). The UN Independent International Commission of Inquiry (Pillay et al.) found extensive war crimes and crimes against humanity in the conduct of Hamas and allied groups on October 7.

The 1988 Hamas Covenant contains explicitly antisemitic language and calls for the destruction of Israel. The 2017 "Document of General Principles and Policies" replaced the religious anti-Jewish framing with a nationalist anti-"Zionist project" framing while continuing to refuse recognition of Israel and continuing to assert claims "from the river to the sea" (Hamas, "A Document of General Principles and Policies," May 1, 2017; 2017 Hamas Charter, Wikipedia summary citing original; Institute for Palestine Studies, "A Newer Hamas?"). Israel and several scholars argue the 2017 document is rhetorical rebranding and that operational policy and post-October 7 statements remain destructive of Israel's existence; others argue the 2017 document reflects a substantive narrowing of the target.

Senior Hamas figures have publicly affirmed the intent to repeat October 7. Khaled Mashal, in a Reuters interview in October 2024, framed October 7 as a "pivotal moment" and affirmed the cyclical character of Palestinian resistance (MEMRI translation of Mashal statements, 2024; Arab Weekly, "Statements of Hamas leader-in-exile draw angry reactions"). Earlier interviews by Ghazi Hamad and others stated October 7 would be repeated "again and again." Scholars including Schabas (in his pre-Gaza writing on Article II of the Convention) and others have noted that statements of intent by a non-state armed group to destroy a protected group (in this framing, Israeli Jews) raise Article II analysis against Hamas itself, independent of how the question runs against Israel (Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2009; Bell and Kontorovich commentary on the bilateral character of Article II analysis, 2024).

## 6. Hamas use of civilian infrastructure: documented record

The record on Hamas's use of civilian infrastructure is partial, contested in places, and verified in others. Cataloguing it accurately is necessary to the counter-position because Israel's IHL argument depends on it.

**Tunnels generally.** Hamas constructed an extensive tunnel network in Gaza, much of it running beneath civilian areas. UN OCHA briefings, IDF documentation, and third-party reporting confirm the existence and scale of the network (IDF Spokesperson briefings; *New York Times* tunnel-mapping investigations, 2024).

**Al-Shifa Hospital.** Israel claimed that Al-Shifa, Gaza's largest hospital, sat above a Hamas command-and-control complex. The IDF released video and stills of weapons, a tunnel shaft, and an underground corridor on the hospital grounds. A *Washington Post* analysis in December 2023 of publicly released material concluded the evidence showed "no immediate evidence of military use by Hamas" in the specific rooms that the IDF had highlighted as connected to a tunnel network (*Washington Post*, "Investigating the assault on al-Shifa," December 21, 2023). A *New York Times* report in February 2024, based on classified Israeli documents, reported that weapons were stored and tunnels existed at the site, although it stated that the evidence Israel had publicly produced did not establish a full command-and-control center (*New York Times*, February 2024). The IDF's December 2024 comments to the OHCHR Fourth Thematic Report on hospitals defended the operation and disputed OHCHR's framing (Israel, "Israel's Initial Comments to OHCHR 4th Thematic Report December 2024," UN unispal archive).

**UNRWA installations.** In February 2024, the IDF reported finding a 700-meter, 18-meter-deep tunnel network beneath UNRWA's Gaza City headquarters, including a Hamas data center; UNRWA Commissioner-General Lazzarini stated UNRWA had vacated the building on October 12, 2023 following an Israeli evacuation order and had not known of the underground infrastructure (Al Jazeera, "UNRWA refutes Israeli implications of Hamas tunnel below Gaza HQ," February 11, 2024; CBC, February 2024; FDD, "Hamas Data Center Found Under UNRWA Headquarters," February 12, 2024). The Colonna Report (April 20, 2024), commissioned by the UN Secretary-General, concluded that UNRWA had a more developed neutrality regime than comparable UN agencies but recommended substantial reforms; UNRWA dismissed several staff alleged to have participated in October 7 (Colonna et al., "Final Report of the Independent Review of Mechanisms and Procedures to Ensure Adherence by UNRWA to the Humanitarian Principle of Neutrality," April 2024). Israel has presented additional documentation of UNRWA staff with Hamas/PIJ affiliations; the *New York Times* in 2024 reported documents indicating senior UNRWA school administrators were Hamas members (*Times of Israel* citing NYT, "NYT: Documents show top administrators at UNRWA schools were Hamas fighters," 2024).

**Schools.** The IDF has presented evidence (photographs, internal Hamas documents) of weapons storage in UNRWA and other schools. UNRWA itself acknowledged in past incidents (predating October 7) that weapons had been found in some of its facilities. The post-October 7 record includes contested instances. Some specific Israeli strike justifications on schools have been disputed by UN agencies and journalists.

**Residential buildings.** IDF documentation shows extensive use of residential buildings as launch sites and weapons storage. Independent verification is uneven; Hamas has acknowledged operations from residential areas in some statements, while disputing specific Israeli identifications of sites.

The honest record is that Hamas's use of civilian infrastructure is established as a general pattern, while specific Israeli identifications of particular sites have been verified in some cases, partially verified in others, and disputed or not yet substantiated in still others. The counter-position relies on the general pattern; the Court will have to assess particular instances.

## 7. Israeli internal pluralism

"Israel's position" is not monolithic. Israeli human rights organizations have produced some of the most rigorous documentation of conduct in Gaza and the West Bank, and several have adopted positions sharply at odds with the government's.

B'Tselem, the longest-established Israeli human rights organization, and Physicians for Human Rights – Israel jointly published "Our Genocide" on July 28, 2025, an 88-page report concluding that "since the Hamas attack on 7 October 2023, Israel has acted in a coordinated and deliberate manner to destroy Palestinian society in the Gaza Strip, committing genocide against its residents" (B'Tselem and PHRI, "Our Genocide," July 28, 2025, btselem.org/publications/202507_our_genocide; B'Tselem press release, July 28, 2025; Amnesty International, "Israel/OPT: Israeli organizations conclude Israel committing genocide," July 2025; CNN, "For first time, two leading Israeli human rights groups accuse Israel of genocide in Gaza," July 28, 2025). The report explicitly invokes the legal framework of the Genocide Convention. It was the first time the two organizations had adopted the legal label.

Adalah, the Legal Center for Arab Minority Rights in Israel, in a letter dated April 8, 2024 to Israel's Attorney General, State Attorney, Police Commissioner and Minister of Justice, demanded criminal investigation of incitement to genocide by Israeli public figures, citing both the Israeli Crime of Genocide (Prevention and Punishment) Law, 5710-1950 and the ICJ's January 26, 2024 provisional measures order (Adalah, "Adalah Demands Israel Investigate and Prosecute Incitement to Genocide by Public Figures," April 8, 2024). Adalah has filed extensive litigation in Israeli courts during the war and has framed elements of Israeli conduct in terms of the Convention.

Yesh Din has, with eleven other Israeli human rights organizations, characterized 2025 as the deadliest and most destructive year for Palestinians since 1967 and has documented West Bank settler violence and IDF conduct in detailed reports. Its public position has stopped short of the genocide label but has used language of "ethnic cleansing" and "crimes against humanity" in joint statements.

The Association for Civil Rights in Israel (ACRI) has been more cautious about the genocide framing but has joined petitions to the Israeli Supreme Court on humanitarian access, detention conditions, and freedom of expression for war critics.

These positions matter to the counter-position section because they show that the strongest version of the Israeli government's legal argument exists in tension with the assessments of significant parts of Israeli civil society. A counter-position section that flattens "Israel" into "the Israeli government" misrepresents the empirical record.
