# Chapter 4. The Preference for Formal Thinking

## The phenomenon

This system measures whether a person prefers to commit their thinking to rigorous, communicable
form. We define **formal thinking** as the commitment of informal thoughts into rigorous,
dimension-reductive frameworks by means of language and logic. We contrast it with **informal
thinking**, which spans the simple and the complex but is, in the end, not rigorous: it includes
the ability to visualize or mentally simulate an outcome from prior observation without ever
formalizing that thought into something communicable.

The crucial point is that this is not a measure of the ability to think formally. It is the
preference to use it. A person fully capable of building a rigorous framework may consistently
prefer not to, working instead in rich informal simulation that they never reduce to language and
logic. We are measuring that preference, not the capacity it declines to use.

## The measures

**1. Preference to formalize.** How readily a person converts an informal thought into a rigorous
framework. The observable is what they reach for when they have a thought worth keeping: do they
press it into a structure, or do they hold it as a simulation?

**2. Preference to communicate the formal product.** Formalization in our sense ends in something
communicable. A person may formalize privately, for their own verification, more or less readily
than they formalize for transmission to others. We record these separately because they come
apart: some formalize only to be understood, others only to be sure.

Both measures record preference. Where preference is hard to separate from capacity, the measure
attends to what a person does with thoughts they could go either way on, not to whether they can
produce a rigorous framework when made to.

## The decision rule

Faced with a thought worth acting on, a person of high formalizing preference reduces it to a
framework before acting and can hand that framework to someone else; a person of low formalizing
preference acts from the informal simulation directly and may be unable, or simply unwilling, to
say why in rigorous terms. Neither is the better thinker by this measure. The measure predicts the
form a person's thinking will take and travel in, not its quality.

## The strategy layer

The strategy here is selective formalization: a person can choose to formalize for some purposes
(persuading, coordinating, checking themselves) and not others. A sophisticated user of this
system formalizes where rigor pays and stays informal where it would only cost time. Whether such
selectivity is itself a stable preference or a learned skill is an open question.

## Illustrations

- High preference to formalize, high preference to communicate. A person who turns thoughts into
  frameworks and frameworks into documents almost by reflex.
- High preference to formalize, low preference to communicate. A person who builds rigorous private
  structures to be sure of their own thinking, but rarely transmits them.
- Low preference to formalize, strong informal simulation. A person who reasons vividly and well in
  their head, reaches sound conclusions, and resists reducing any of it to a communicable
  framework.

## Open questions

- Are the two measures (formalize, communicate) one preference or two?
- Does this system entangle with observation fidelity from Chapter 3? High-fidelity informal
  simulation may substitute for formalization.
- Is selective formalization a strategy within this system, or a sign of a separate system about
  how a person allocates effort?
