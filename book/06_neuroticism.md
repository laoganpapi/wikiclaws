# Chapter 6. Neuroticism

## The phenomenon

This system measures baseline emotional stability: how steady or how volatile a person is, and how
their reactivity changes under load. It is the system that tells you what happens to the rest of
the machinery when the pressure comes on. A person's social economy, thinking machine, and
emotional competence all run differently when that person is stable than when they are reactive,
and neuroticism is the dimension that captures that difference.

Neuroticism is distinct from emotional intelligence. Emotional competence (Chapter 4) is about
attention and handling in the emotional channel; it is, in part, a skill. Neuroticism is about
*baseline reactivity*: how readily and how strongly a person's emotional system fires, and how
long it takes to settle. A person can be highly emotionally intelligent and highly neurotic, or
stable and emotionally oblivious. The two are correlated in the population but are not the same
axis, and the assessment keeps them apart.

## The measures

**1. Baseline stability versus volatility.** Where a person sits when nothing in particular is
wrong: even and steady at one pole, easily and strongly moved at the other.

**2. Reactivity under load.** How far and how fast stability degrades as pressure rises. Two people
with the same calm baseline can diverge sharply under stress: one holds, the other spikes. This
second measure is the one that matters most for the rest of the framework, because it is the
mechanism by which neuroticism reaches into the other systems.

Both are read mainly through revealed response to pressure rather than self-description, since
people are unreliable narrators of their own steadiness. The instrument observes behavior across
its lower- and higher-stakes scenarios and reads stability from the difference.

## The decision rule

Under a stressor, a stable person's other systems keep running near their unpressured settings; a
reactive person's systems are pulled off those settings by the emotional response. Neuroticism
does not, by itself, dictate a decision; it dictates how much the decision-making apparatus is
disturbed while the decision is being made.

## The dynamic layer

This is the system's most important content, and it is a cross-system claim. Neuroticism is, in
large part, the **moderator of every other system's stake response.** When the thinking machine
migrates across its plane or collapses from slow to fast as stakes rise, how sharply it does so is
partly a neuroticism story. When emotional attention narrows under pressure in Chapter 4, the rate
of narrowing is partly a neuroticism story. When the social economy's risk appetite swings in a
high-pressure interaction, neuroticism conditions the swing. For this reason, neuroticism is read
not only on its own scenarios but also through the *steepness* of the stake responses observed in
the other systems. It is the system most entangled with the others, by design, and that entanglement
is part of what a reviewer should examine when testing whether the systems are genuinely separable.

## Illustrations

- Stable baseline, low reactivity under load. Other systems hold their settings whether the stakes
  are trivial or severe; the person under pressure looks much like the person at rest.
- Stable baseline, high reactivity under load. Calm until it counts, then pulled hard off settings;
  the gap between the rested and pressured person is wide.
- Volatile baseline, moderate reactivity. Easily moved in ordinary life, but the additional
  disturbance from rising stakes is comparatively small, because the system is already near its
  ceiling.

## Open questions

- Are baseline stability and reactivity under load two measures or one? They can diverge, which
  argues for two, but they may prove hard to separate in practice.
- How much of neuroticism should be measured directly, and how much inferred from the steepness of
  the other systems' stake responses?
- Given a young population, how do we distinguish trait reactivity from the ordinary volatility of
  a developmental stage, so that a reading is not mistaken for a verdict?
