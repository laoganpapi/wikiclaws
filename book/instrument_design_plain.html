The idea in one paragraph. Most personality tests ask you what you're like and trust your answer. This one mostly watches what you do in small, realistic situations, because research shows people are honest but often wrong about themselves. There are no right answers anywhere, and nothing is scored for being smart. The test only records which way you lean: gut or deliberation, your own hunches or outside facts, keeping the peace or pressing the point, and what you're actually chasing in life.

AThe Arena — how you think when it counts

Fifteen short decision scenarios: picking between job offers, handling a group project going sideways, spending or saving a windfall. Some demand a snap answer; some give you time. And each one tells you up front how likely it is that the AI will ask you to explain your answer afterward: never, maybe, or definitely. That "will I have to defend this?" pressure is how we turn the dial on what's at stake, and what we watch is whether your style of thinking changes as the pressure rises.

Example: a snap item

"Two clubs accepted you and the fair closes in one minute. Robotics club: your two best friends joined, meets Tuesdays. Debate club: a teacher said you'd be great at it, meets Thursdays. Read as long as you like. When you tap 'ready', you'll have 5 seconds to pick."

Before the clock runs out, one bonus tap: you may peek at one thing: last year's member survey results, or a one-line reminder of what you said matters to you. Which peek you grab, under a 5-second clock, quietly tells us whether your instinct reaches for outside facts or your own compass.

What we learn: where your gut goes when there's no time to dress it up.

Example: a slow item

"Your team's app demo is in two weeks and one teammate has gone quiet. You have up to four minutes to decide what to do."

On screen: three flip-cards of information you may open (the teammate's recent messages, how past teams handled this, what the judges reward), and an optional pros-and-cons grid you can fill in or ignore. Open all the cards or none. Use the grid or wing it. Then say what you'd do and why.

What we learn: given time, do you gather outside evidence or reason it out yourself; do you build a structured case or trust a feel for it. If you fill the grid one way and choose the opposite, we notice that too.

A few items are gentle honesty checks. Two versions of the same scenario exist where a hidden detail points opposite directions; if someone's "snap" answers keep tracking a detail they shouldn't have had time to find, the snap answers weren't snap.

BThe Ledger — where your social energy goes

No quiz about whether you're an introvert. Instead the AI walks you through yesterday, episode by episode: what you did, who was there, and how charged or drained you felt at each step. Your energy pattern is computed from your actual day, because people's beliefs about "people drain me" are famously less accurate than their memory of specific moments.

Example: rebuilding yesterday

"Walk me through after school. You said practice, then the bus, then homework. At practice, who were you with? And right then, was your battery filling up or running down?"

What we learn: whether company charges or drains you, which company, and how fast you recover; from lived episodes, not self-image.

Example: a social risk moment

"Your close friend keeps showing up late to your shared shift and you're covering for them. Write the message you would actually send them."

What we learn: do you raise the hard thing at all, how directly, and how much relationship you're willing to put on the line. Judged only on what you chose to say, never on how nicely you wrote it.

CThe Room — you, in charged moments

Short social situations with real tension, plus two brief staged moments inside the session itself (the consent form says so up front: "the AI will sometimes disagree with you or be confusing on purpose", and it always explains afterward). If anything causes real distress, the probe stops and that part simply isn't scored.

Example: a scenario

"In the group chat, someone screenshots your friend's awkward post and everyone's piling on. What do you do? And, honestly: in that moment, what would you be paying the most attention to?"

What we learn: whether you smooth things over or push back, and whether your attention goes to your own reaction or to reading the room.

Example: a staged moment

Midway through, the AI politely challenges one of your earlier answers: "I'm not sure that follows. Here's why." Do you push back, fold, or meet it halfway? Later, one task arrives weirdly worded, then gets fixed. How thrown were you, and how fast did you settle?

What we learn: how you handle being contradicted, and how steady you stay when something rubs wrong. Because you were warned it might happen, we treat "stayed calm" as the floor of your steadiness, not the ceiling.

DThe Compass — what you're actually chasing

First, in your own words: what are you working toward, and why does that matter? The AI asks "why" a couple of times, because first answers are usually goals (get into a program) and the real motive lives underneath (what the program is for). Then a series of either-or choices where the motives are dressed in ordinary situations, and finally one real choice about your own results.

Example: an either-or

"Two summer roles, same pay. One: you'd run the volunteer schedule; people would do things your way. Two: you'd get to find out why the program keeps losing volunteers; nobody may act on what you find. Pick one."

What we learn: influence versus understanding, when they're in direct trade. Sixteen of these, each pitting two drives against each other, worded so the "impressive" answer isn't obvious.

Example: the last choice

"You're done. One choice left: which page of your report do you want first? The thing you're best at. The hardest truth we found. How you compare to your cohort. Or the page we haven't shown anyone yet."

One real decision about yourself, at the very end, when the test is "over". It's small, but it's honest.

✉What comes back — the report

No overall score, no type label, no pass or fail. Each of the four areas gets its own page, in sentences like:

Example lines from a report

"On instinct, you read the situation in front of you and go with feel. Given time and pressure, you switch: you reach for structure and your own principles. Most people don't switch that cleanly; it's worth knowing which mode you're in when it matters."

"You describe yourself as someone drained by people. Your actual day said something more specific: small, familiar company charged you; the crowd drained you. The label 'introvert' is costing you the first half of that sentence."

Hard promises attached to every report

  • Results are for the person's own development until the test has proven it predicts real outcomes; nobody gets hired or rejected off it.
  • How steady someone is under pressure, and gaps between self-image and behavior, are never shared for selection decisions.
  • The person controls who sees their report. Minors' results expire after 18 months, because a reading at 16 is a reading of 16.
  • For minors: guardian consent, a plain-language walkthrough afterward of the staged moments, and the right to stop anytime with no penalty.

✓How we'll know it works

  • It must not be an IQ test in disguise. Trial participants also take a standard reasoning test; our measures must show close to zero relationship with it. That's the single number we most want to show a skeptical scientist.
  • The snap answers must really be snap. If "fast" and "slow" answers turn out to be the same thing measured twice, we say so and simplify the test rather than pretend.
  • The AI's reading of written answers must match human experts, and must not secretly reward good writing; we check it against people who write plainly or in a second language.
  • Same person, month later, similar picture. A fresh set of scenarios, four to six weeks on; if a result doesn't hold, it gets reported at group level only, not pinned on individuals.
