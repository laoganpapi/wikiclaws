# Chapter 3. The Thinking Machine

## The phenomenon

This is the central system of the theory and the one the assessment is built around. It governs
how a person thinks: where they draw their thinking from, how rigorously they form it, how fast
they run it, and, above all, how all of that moves as the stakes rise. Most accounts of thinking
style fix a person at a point. The claim here is that a person is not a point. A person is a
trajectory across a space, and the shape of that trajectory under pressure is more telling than
any single coordinate.

We build the system from three parts: a plane with two axes, a modal layer laid over it, and a
modulator that sets the whole thing in motion.

## The measures

### The plane: two axes

**Axis A. Internal versus External sourcing.** When a person reasons, do they draw on their own
internal models, theories, and priors, or on external empirical data and the feedback of the
world? We frame this as theory versus evidence. The internal thinker reasons from a model they
already hold; the external thinker reaches for data and lets it move them. This is a preference
over the *source* of thought, not the quality of it.

**Axis B. Formal versus Informal method.** Does a person commit a thought to a rigorous,
dimension-reductive framework expressed in language and logic, or do they work informally, in
visualization and mental simulation that is not reduced to communicable form? Informal thinking
spans the simple and the complex; what makes it informal is that it is not made rigorous and
not made transmissible. This is a preference over the *form* of thought.

Together the two axes define a plane with four corners, which the assessment can name back to a
person in plain terms:

- **Internal and Formal:** building from first principles; deductive theory.
- **Internal and Informal:** intuition, imagination, private simulation from one's own models.
- **External and Formal:** empirical and statistical rigor; structured reading of data.
- **External and Informal:** situational reading; practical, in-the-moment sense of the world.

A person sits somewhere in this plane, not necessarily in a corner.

### The modal layer: fast and slow

Over the plane we lay a second distinction, taken from Kahneman: the **fast system** of automatic,
low-effort, pattern-matched thinking, and the **slow system** of effortful, deliberate reasoning.
We use this as a modeling layer for an observed fact, that people switch between an automatic mode
and a deliberate one, and not as a claim about the architecture of the brain. The dual-process
account is contested in the literature, and we lean on it only as far as the observed switching
supports.

The modal layer is close to orthogonal to the plane. A person can be fast and informal (a gut
read), fast and formal (an expert applying a memorized framework without apparent effort), slow
and informal (a long, unhurried daydream of how something might play out), or slow and formal
(working a problem through on paper). Because the two are separable, we measure them separately.

For the modal layer we measure **strength**, not only preference: how strong a person's fast
system is, and how strong their slow system is. This is the one place in the theory where we
deliberately measure a capacity. We do it because the dynamic story below cannot be told without
it: you cannot describe how a person switches modes under pressure if you do not know how much
mode they have to switch into. We mark the crossing here, and we owe a reviewer an account, given
in the open questions, of how slow-system strength is to be kept distinct from general
intelligence.

### The modulator: stakes

The last part is what sets the system in motion. **Stakes** are how much a person has to gain or
lose in the situation they are thinking about. The core claim of the thinking machine is that a
person's position in the plane and their mode are not fixed; they are functions of the stakes. As
stakes rise, people move, and they move in characteristic ways.

## The decision rule and the dynamic layer

The output of this system is not a coordinate. It is a trajectory: where a person sits in the
plane and which mode they run at low stakes, and how both migrate as stakes climb. Some
characteristic trajectories:

- Under low stakes a person reasons externally and informally, reading the room by feel; as stakes
  rise they retreat inward to a formal framework they trust, trading the room for the model.
- Under low stakes a person runs the slow, deliberate system; as stakes rise the slow system
  collapses and the fast system takes over, for better or worse.
- A person who stays put: same coordinates, same mode, whether the stakes are trivial or
  enormous. Stability is itself a finding.

The assessment therefore has to evaluate four things and how they interact:

1. The strength of the fast system and the strength of the slow system.
2. The default and range on the Formal-Informal axis.
3. The default and range on the Internal-External axis.
4. The stake response: how 2 and 3 shift, and which mode engages, as stakes are raised.

### How it can be measured

The instrument presents scenarios and varies the stakes within them, raising what the person
stands to gain or lose and observing what changes. From a person's responses, an AI can code two
things that are otherwise hard to score at scale: whether a response is formal (does it build a
rigorous, communicable framework?) or informal (does it rest on simulation and feel?), and whether
it is internal (does it argue from the person's own model?) or external (does it seek, cite, or
defer to data?). Fast and slow are inferred from process: response time, whether the person
revises, and their own account of how they arrived at the answer. The design is adaptive: raise
the stakes until a shift in coordinates or mode appears, then record where and how it appeared.
The coding must be auditable and checked for bias, since coding free text by writing quality
rather than thinking style is a real failure mode, and the population is young and linguistically
diverse.

## Illustrations

- Strong slow system, weak fast system, Internal-Formal by default, stable under stakes. A person
  who reasons from principles, slowly and rigorously, and does not abandon the method when it
  matters most.
- Strong fast system, External-Informal by default, but migrates to Internal-Formal as stakes
  rise. A person who reads situations quickly and practically in low-stakes life, then reaches for
  a trusted framework and slows down when the cost of error grows.
- Comparable fast and slow strength, External across the board, but flips from Informal to Formal
  as stakes rise. A person who trusts the world's data throughout and only formalizes their reading
  of it when something is on the line.

## Open questions

- **Slow-system strength versus fluid intelligence.** This is the first thing a cognitive
  scientist will press. The intended answer is that we measure the *disposition to engage* the
  slow system and the *quality of mode-switching* under stakes, not raw deliberative horsepower;
  the chapter and the instrument must hold that line, and we should test whether our measure
  separates from a standard working-memory or reasoning measure.
- Is stakes one modulator or several? Gain and loss may move a person differently; social stakes
  may move them differently from material or moral stakes.
- Are the two plane axes truly orthogonal, or does formality rise with internal sourcing because
  one's own models are easier to formalize than raw data?
- Does the Internal-External axis here separate cleanly from situational awareness in System 3?
- How stable is a person's trajectory over time, given that personality is not static?
