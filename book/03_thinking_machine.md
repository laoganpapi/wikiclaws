# Chapter 3. The Thinking Machine

## The phenomenon

This is the central system of the theory and the one the assessment is built around. It governs
how a person thinks: where they source their thinking, how rigorously they form it, and how that
reliance shifts as the stakes of a task rise.

That a person is not a fixed point, but a set of if-then responses that shift with the situation,
is not our discovery. Social-cognitive personality research established it decades ago: Mischel
and Shoda's situation-behavior signatures, Fleeson's density distributions, and the whole-trait
program that followed all measured personality as situation-contingent variability, not as a
single score. We inherit that result and do not claim it. Our contribution is narrower and
practical: a one-session, auditable way to parameterize how a specific person's cognitive
reliance moves across two axes (sourcing and method) as mode and stakes change, in a plane the
thinking-style literature has mostly measured only as static points, and to do it in ninety
minutes rather than the weeks of repeated sampling the older work required. The claim is an
instrument claim, not a claim to have discovered that people are situation-dependent.

We are explicit about what we do not measure. We do not measure the strength of a person's
thinking, fast or slow. Strength is capacity, capacity is the province of intelligence, and this
theory stays out of it. We measure reliance: which kind of thinking a person leans on, given the
mode they are in and the stakes they face.

The system has three parts: a plane defined by two axes, two modes of operation laid over it, and
a modulator that drives the reliance from one part of the plane toward another.

## The measures

### The plane: two axes of reliance

**Axis A. Cognitive sourcing: Internal versus External.** When a person reasons, do they lean on
their own internal models, theories, and priors, or on external empirical data and the feedback of
the world? We frame this as theory versus evidence. We call it cognitive sourcing to mark its
boundary against the emotional and social attention measured in the next system; this axis is
about where reasoning draws its raw material, not about where emotional attention points.

**Axis B. Method: Formal versus Informal.** Does a person commit a thought to a rigorous,
dimension-reductive framework expressed in language and logic, or work informally, in
visualization and mental simulation not reduced to communicable form? Informal thinking spans the
simple and the complex; what makes it informal is that it is not made rigorous and not made
transmissible.

Together the axes define a plane with four corners, which the assessment can name back to a person
in plain terms:

- **Internal and Formal:** building from first principles; deductive theory.
- **Internal and Informal:** intuition, imagination, private simulation from one's own models.
- **External and Formal:** empirical and statistical rigor; structured reading of data.
- **External and Informal:** situational reading; practical, in-the-moment sense of the world.

A person does not own a corner. A person leans somewhere in this plane, and the lean moves.

### The modes: fast and slow

Over the plane we lay a distinction taken from Kahneman: the **fast system** of automatic,
low-effort, pattern-matched thinking, and the **slow system** of effortful, deliberate reasoning.
We use this as a modeling layer for an observed fact, that people operate sometimes in an
automatic mode and sometimes in a deliberate one, and not as a claim about the architecture of the
brain. The dual-process account is contested, and we lean on it only as far as the observed
switching supports.

Crucially, fast and slow are not things we score for strength. They are two **conditions** under
which we observe a person's reliance, and the instrument elicits each: a fast condition under time
pressure and a demand for a snap judgment, a slow condition with room to deliberate. The question
is never how powerful a person's fast or slow system is. The question is, when thinking fast, what
do they lean on, and when thinking slow, what do they lean on, and how does each lean move with the
stakes.

### The modulator: stakes

**Stakes** are how much a person has to gain or lose in the situation they are thinking about. The
core claim of the thinking machine is that reliance is a function of stakes. As stakes rise,
people lean differently, and they lean in characteristic ways.

## The decision rule and the output

The output of this system is not a coordinate and not a strength score. It is **two graphs**.

- **The fast-system graph.** Stakes on the horizontal axis. Two trend lines: the person's weight
  on the Formal-Informal axis, and their weight on the Internal-External axis, each plotted as the
  stakes rise, while the person is thinking in the fast mode.
- **The slow-system graph.** The same two trend lines, observed while the person is thinking in the
  slow mode.

Read together, the two graphs say where a person leans when they think fast and when they think
slow, and how both leanings travel as the cost of the task climbs. Some characteristic shapes:

- In the fast graph, External and Informal at low stakes, migrating toward Internal and Formal as
  stakes rise: a person whose snap reads start with the room and retreat to a trusted model when it
  matters.
- A flat slow graph: a person who, given room to deliberate, leans the same way regardless of
  stakes. Stability is itself a finding.
- Fast and slow graphs that diverge sharply: a person who is one kind of thinker on instinct and a
  different kind on reflection, which tells you which mode to put them in for which task.

### How it is measured

The instrument presents scenarios and varies the stakes within them, raising what the person
stands to gain or lose. It elicits responses under both conditions: a fast condition that demands a
quick answer, and a slow condition that invites deliberation. From each response, an AI codes two
things that are otherwise hard to score at scale: whether the response is formal (does it build a
rigorous, communicable framework?) or informal (does it rest on simulation and feel?), and whether
it is internal (does it argue from the person's own model?) or external (does it seek, cite, or
defer to data?). Plotting those codes against stakes, for each mode, produces the two graphs. The
coding must be auditable and checked for bias, since coding free text by writing quality rather
than by thinking style is a real failure mode, and the population is young and linguistically
diverse.

## Illustrations

- Fast graph migrates External-Informal toward Internal-Formal as stakes rise; slow graph sits
  steadily Internal-Formal throughout. On instinct this person reads the room and then reaches for
  their model under pressure; given time, they always reason from principle.
- Both graphs stay External across all stakes, but both flip Informal to Formal as stakes rise.
  This person trusts the world's data whether fast or slow, and only formalizes their reading of it
  when something is on the line.
- Fast and slow graphs nearly identical and nearly flat. This person leans the same way regardless
  of mode or stakes; what you see at rest is what you get under fire.

## Open questions

- **Strength versus reliance, resolved by design.** Earlier drafts measured the strength of the
  fast and slow systems, which invited the objection that slow-system strength is merely fluid
  intelligence. We have retired that measurement. We measure only reliance, conditional on mode and
  stakes. The residual question for a reviewer is narrower: can the instrument elicit a genuine
  fast condition and a genuine slow condition cleanly enough that the two graphs are distinct
  measurements rather than the same one taken twice?
- Are the two plane axes truly orthogonal, or does formality rise with internal sourcing because
  one's own models are easier to formalize than raw data?
- Does the Internal-External cognitive-sourcing axis separate cleanly from the emotional and social
  attention of System 3?
- How stable are the two graphs over time, given that personality is not static?
