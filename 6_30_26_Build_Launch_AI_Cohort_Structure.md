# Build+Launch AI

*Pilot Cohort: Structure, Program, and Process. Working draft from Alex Ye.*

Confidential. 6/30/26.

---

## Container

The pilot runs as a **2-week summer intensive**: 10 half-day sessions (mornings, roughly 3 hours each, about 30 contact hours), ending in a demo day. Mornings in the room, afternoons free for student user-outreach and life. This maximizes momentum and demo-day output while preserving the calendar time an intensive otherwise starves: the days users need to reply to interview requests.

Grade band is **8 through 12**. That spread is wide, so it gets handled in how teams are built rather than treated as one homogeneous room.

---

## 1. Cohort Parameters

**Cohort size: 12 students.**
Small enough that one lead and two coaches can mentor every team daily, large enough to field three or four teams and create real peer energy. A 1:4 staff-to-student ratio is the floor for guided building. Hold 12 for the pilot, with 16 as the ceiling only if demand and a second coach both materialize.

**Teams: three or four students, mixed grade, four teams total.**
Each team anchors on an older student (11th or 12th) who carries more autonomy and naturally absorbs the de facto lead role, paired with younger members who get pulled up rather than left behind. Mixed-grade teams turn the age spread from a pacing problem into a mentorship structure inside each group.

**Staffing: one lead facilitator, two floating coaches, a mentor pool.**
The lead runs the daily arc and framework bursts. Coaches circulate during work blocks, unblock teams, and never lecture. The mentor pool (founders, operators, investors from the founding team's network) shows up for two office-hours windows and demo-day judging, not daily.

**Recruitment: personal networks and referrals, parents looped in at sign-up.**
Pilot fills through warm channels, so cohort fit and parent buy-in come pre-loaded. Sign-up captures the student's interest areas, which seeds team formation on day one.

## 2. Program Arc

The arc moves through six beats: frame, find a problem, validate it with real users, build with AI, refine, pitch. Week one earns the right to build by proving a problem is real. Week two builds and sells.

### Week 1: Frame and Validate

1. **Day 1, Orient and Form.** What this is and is not, the build-and-pitch journey ahead, team formation by interest area, each team picks a problem space. Outreach starts today: every student lists five people they could interview and sends the first messages before leaving.
2. **Day 2, Problem Discovery.** Fall in love with the problem, not the solution. Interview technique (open questions, no pitching, listen for pain). Teams draft and send their interview guide.
3. **Day 3, Validate.** Read out interview signal, separate real pain from assumed pain, narrow to one problem and one clearly named user. Teams that found no real problem pivot now, on purpose.
4. **Day 4, Solution and AI Tooling.** Ideate solutions against the validated problem, then meet AI as the leverage that lets a four-person team build something real in a week. Scope a minimum version: the smallest thing that proves the idea.
5. **Day 5, Build Kickoff and Checkpoint.** Building starts. First mentor office hours. Rough checkpoint pitch to the room (two minutes, no slides) to surface gaps while there is still time to fix them.

### Week 2: Build and Pitch

1. **Day 6, Build.** Heads-down prototyping with AI, coaches unblocking.
2. **Day 7, Build and First Real Feedback.** Get the working prototype in front of an actual user, watch them use it, iterate on what breaks.
3. **Day 8, Refine and Story.** Pitch narrative frameworks: problem, who hurts, what you built, what happened when a real person used it. Slide skeleton drafted.
4. **Day 9, Rehearsal.** Full dry run, mentor feedback, polish. Every student speaks to part of their team's pitch.
5. **Day 10, Demo Day.** Teams present to a panel of founders, operators, and investors. Authentic feedback, recognition, parents in the room.

## 3. Operating Process

**Daily session shape (the repeatable rhythm).**
Open with a 15-minute standup: each team states yesterday's progress and today's one goal. Then the core work block, where coaches circulate. Close with a 15-minute share-out and a written commitment for the next session. The same shape every day means students stop spending energy on what happens next and spend it on the work.

**Facilitation model: guides, not lectures.**
Frameworks arrive just-in-time in bursts under 15 minutes, attached to the moment a team needs them, never as front-loaded curriculum. Everything else is doing, with coaches asking questions rather than giving answers. The room is a workshop, not a classroom.

**Frameworks taught (kept deliberately few).**
Problem framing, the user interview, minimum-version scoping, and pitch narrative. Four tools, each taught once at the moment of use. Anything beyond these competes with build time and gets cut.

**Feedback loops.**
Daily close-out surfaces blockers inside the cohort. Two mentor office-hours windows (Day 5 and Day 9) bring outside eyes. A mid-point parent note and a post-program parent and student interview close the loop with families, which is where pilot validation actually lives.

**Parent engagement.**
Orientation note at sign-up setting expectations, a short mid-point update, the demo-day invitation, and an exit interview. Parents are the pilot's primary validators, so each touch is also a listening post.

**Demo day mechanics.**
Four teams, six minutes each (four to present, two for judge questions). Judges are working founders, operators, and investors. Recognition is real feedback and visible acknowledgment, not a ranked competition that turns teammates into rivals. The bar for a successful pilot is four teams that each present a working, AI-built prototype tied to a problem a real person confirmed.

**Artifacts each team leaves with.**
A one-sentence problem statement, evidence from real user interviews, a working prototype, a pitch deck, and a recorded demo. These are what a parent shows a relative and what the academy shows the next cohort's families.

## Open Questions

- **Venue and exact dates.** Two consecutive weeks, location that supports four teams plus power and wifi.
- **AI tool stack.** Which tools we standardize on, and how we provision accounts for minors safely.
- **Hardware.** School-provided laptops, BYOD, or loaners.
- **Final cohort size.** Hold at 12, or stretch to 16 if a second coach is confirmed.
- **Judge recruitment.** Locking three to five demo-day judges from the founding network.
- **Pricing.** Whether the pilot is free, deliberately, to remove a barrier and buy candid feedback.
- **Consent and liability.** Parental consent, minor data handling, and standard waivers before day one.
