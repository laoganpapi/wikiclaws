**Reviewer:** Adversarial Red Team 4 of 7 (International Criminal Law / Public International Law)

**Date:** 25 May 2026

## 1. Executive Summary

The synthesis and brief are, on the whole, doctrinally creditable. Article II is quoted accurately. The *Bosnia* ¶373 and *Croatia* ¶148 formulations of the "only reasonable inference" test are reproduced verbatim and correctly characterised as governing inference from pattern of conduct. The "fully conclusive" merits standard (*Bosnia* ¶209; *Croatia* ¶178) is correctly distinguished from the procedural plausibility standard at provisional measures (*Gambia v. Myanmar* ¶¶30, 56; *South Africa v. Israel* ¶¶30, 54). The *Akayesu* ¶¶504, 731–734 threshold for Article II(b) and the *Akayesu* ¶¶505–506 catalogue for Article II(c) are correctly stated. *Krstić*'s "substantial part" doctrine (Appeals Judgment, 19 April 2004, ¶¶8–14) is reproduced.

There are, however, several doctrinal points where the synthesis either omits controlling authority, blurs categorical distinctions that the case law treats as separate, or fails to register an open scholarly disagreement that the reader should not be insulated from. These are catalogued below.

The single most consequential doctrinal finding: **the synthesis collapses three analytically distinct State-responsibility tracks under the Convention (the obligation not to commit under Article I and the chapeau of Article II; the obligation to prevent under Article I; and the obligation not to be complicit under Article III(e)) into an undifferentiated "is it genocide" question, with the result that the duty-to-prevent claim in the South Africa pleadings and the Albanese "63 complicit States" framing are not given their own doctrinal architecture.** *Bosnia v. Serbia* ¶¶425–438 (duty to prevent) and ¶¶419–424 (complicity) are controlling on those tracks and are not cited. (Counts below: HIGH 4, MEDIUM 7, LOW 4.)

## 2. HIGH-Severity Findings

### H1. Duty to prevent (Article I) is doctrinally unbriefed.

The synthesis at §3 reports that the Court's 26 January 2024 Order "indicated six provisional measures, including the requirement that Israel 'take all measures within its power to prevent the commission' of acts within the scope of Article II." This tracks the Order. But the synthesis nowhere identifies that the Article I obligation to prevent is a distinct, free-standing obligation, breach of which does not require a finding of commission. *Bosnia v. Serbia* (26 February 2007), ¶¶425–438, is the controlling authority: the duty to prevent "is a distinct obligation, separate from the duty to punish" (¶427); the duty arises "at the instant that the State learns of, or should normally have learned of, the existence of a serious risk that genocide will be committed" (¶431); and the standard of liability is "due diligence" (¶430). The Court found Serbia in breach of the duty to prevent in respect of Srebrenica notwithstanding its acquittal on the principal charge of commission (¶438, dispositif (5)).

This matters in three places the synthesis does not connect:

- The South Africa Application's prayer for relief and Memorial include duty-to-prevent claims, not only duty-not-to-commit claims. The synthesis at §3 should register this.
- The provisional-measures architecture in *South Africa v. Israel* tracks the duty-to-prevent framework directly: the Court's order that Israel "take all measures within its power to prevent the commission" of Article II acts is anchored in Article I, and rests on the *Bosnia* ¶431 "serious risk" trigger.
- The "complicit States" framing (see H2) is structurally adjacent to the duty-to-prevent track, but distinct from it.

The synthesis's silence on Article I as a separately actionable obligation creates a misimpression that the only path to State responsibility runs through commission. That is doctrinally incorrect under controlling ICJ jurisprudence.

**Recommendation:** add a paragraph in §2 ("The Legal Standard") and a paragraph in §7(b) registering Article I as a distinct obligation under *Bosnia* ¶¶425–438, including the "due diligence" liability standard and the "serious risk" trigger.

### H2. State-as-perpetrator and State-as-complicit are conflated in the treatment of the Albanese "63 complicit States" framing.

The synthesis at §6 ("UN bodies") reports that Albanese's October 2025 report "identif[ies] 63 complicit states." The synthesis does not separately identify the doctrinal architecture of complicity in genocide. *Bosnia v. Serbia* ¶¶419–424 is controlling. The Court held that Article III(e) complicity in the Convention "is similar to" but not identical with the ARSIWA Article 16 standard of aid or assistance in the commission of an internationally wrongful act; the Court applied ARSIWA 16's "knowledge" element to State complicity in genocide and rejected a "specific intent on the part of the complicit State" requirement (¶421). The Court further held that "at the least, the organs of the accomplice State knew" of the genocidal intent of the principal (¶422).

The synthesis does not register:

- That the Article III(e) standard for State complicity, as articulated by the *Bosnia* Court, applies "knowledge" rather than "specific intent" to the complicit State (¶421);
- That, conversely, individual criminal complicity in genocide at the ICTY/ICTR has at times been held to require knowledge of the principal's specific intent (cf. *Krstić*, Appeals Judgment, 19 April 2004, ¶¶140–142, treating aiding and abetting genocide; the doctrinal posture differs from State complicity);
- That under this architecture, an Article III(e) finding against a third State (Albanese's framing) is logically dependent on a prior finding of commission of genocide by Israel, but conceptually distinct in its mental element.

Folding this into a single "complicit states" line in the matrix obscures the doctrinal architecture that any merits adjudication of the Albanese framing would require. Schabas (*Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., 2009, ch. 9) and Ambos (*Treatise on International Criminal Law*, vol. II, 2014, §1.B) provide the relevant scholarly treatment.

**Recommendation:** in §2 of the synthesis, distinguish (i) State-as-perpetrator (Article II commission attributed via ILC Arts. 4–11); (ii) State-as-complicit (Article III(e) under the *Bosnia* ¶¶419–424 knowledge standard); (iii) State-as-failure-to-prevent (Article I, *Bosnia* ¶¶425–438 due diligence). The Albanese framing falls in (ii).

### H3. The ICC Elements of Crimes "manifest pattern" contextual element is not engaged.

The synthesis at §4(c) and the brief at §4(c) discuss the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber's findings on conditions of life without engaging the fact that Article 6 of the ICC Elements of Crimes attaches a contextual element to each of Article 6(a)–(e): the conduct must have taken place "in the context of a manifest pattern of similar conduct directed against that group or was conduct that could itself effect such destruction" (Elements of Crimes, Art. 6, common chapeau element 4). Whether this introduces a substantive contextual requirement absent from the Convention text is contested. Schabas treats it as "the most controversial provision of the Elements" and reads it as a procedural threshold (Schabas, *The International Criminal Court: A Commentary on the Rome Statute*, 2nd ed., 2016, pp. 130–135). Ambos reads it as substantive (Ambos, *Treatise*, vol. II, 2014, §1.A.II). Mettraux treats it as importing an objective dimension that may be in tension with the purely subjective dolus specialis (Mettraux, *International Crimes: Law and Practice*, vol. I, 2019, §3.4).

This omission has two specific consequences for the synthesis:

- The Pre-Trial Chamber's decision not to charge under Article 6 of the Rome Statute, despite using Article II(c)-resonant language under the CAH framing, is doctrinally explicable in part by reference to the "manifest pattern" contextual requirement at the warrant stage. The synthesis presents the ICC's choice as an unexplained datum.
- The "in the context of a manifest pattern" language has produced its own micro-debate (Schabas–Ambos–Mettraux–Kress) that the synthesis's engagement with the doctrinal-debate landscape should register.

**Recommendation:** add a paragraph in §2 of the synthesis after the Article II(c) treatment, identifying the Elements of Crimes contextual element and the contested doctrinal status (Schabas vs. Ambos camp).

### H4. The synthesis's framing of ICC Pre-Trial Chamber language as "Article II(c)-resonant" risks suggesting the PTC made findings under Article II.

The synthesis at §§4(c), 6, and the brief at §4(c) describe the ICC Pre-Trial Chamber I's 21 November 2024 finding that "reasonable grounds to believe" the conditions of life were "calculated to bring about the destruction of part of the civilian population in Gaza." The synthesis characterises this as "Article II(c)-resonant language used in its IHL/CAH framing rather than under Article 6." The phrase "Article II(c)-resonant" is doctrinally precise but the surrounding text trades on it. The Pre-Trial Chamber did not charge Article 6 of the Rome Statute. The "conditions of life" language in the warrant operates under the war crime of starvation (Rome Statute, Art. 8(2)(b)(xxv); on the ICC's accepted reading post-Amendment in IAC) and the crimes against humanity of murder, persecution, and other inhumane acts (Art. 7). The substantive elements differ from Article 6(c) genocide in two respects: there is no group-as-such requirement, and there is no dolus specialis. The "calculated to bring about the destruction" formulation is, in the warrant context, descriptive of the actus reus of starvation as a method of warfare and of the actus reus / contextual element of the CAH counts, not a finding under genocide doctrine.

The synthesis correctly notes that the ICC did not charge Article 6. But the phrase "Article II(c)-resonant language" reads, to a lay reader, like a partial finding of (c). Doctrinally there is no partial finding. The PTC found "reasonable grounds to believe" under Rome Statute Article 58(1), which is a separate evidentiary threshold for a separate substantive offence.

**Recommendation:** the synthesis and brief should expressly state that the PTC's language operates under Articles 7 and 8 of the Rome Statute and that it is not, doctrinally, an Article II finding or a finding partially establishing Article II(c). The current phrasing in §6 of the synthesis (the matrix row "ICC at the warrant stage sits in row (d), with Article II(c)-resonant language") is fine for the matrix; the §4(c) discussion should be tightened.

## 3. MEDIUM-Severity Findings

### M1. "Fully conclusive" is presented as settled to be higher than beyond-reasonable-doubt; this is contested.

Agent 01 at §4 says "'Fully conclusive' is the highest standard the ICJ has articulated in inter-State proceedings, higher than 'balance of probabilities' and exceeding 'clear and convincing evidence'" (citing Schabas 2009, Mettraux 2019). The synthesis adopts the formulation without registering that Mettraux himself notes the unresolved question whether "fully conclusive" is materially more demanding than beyond-reasonable-doubt or "merely the same threshold in different language" (Mettraux, *International Crimes*, vol. I, 2019, pp. 290–305; reproduced in agent 01 §4 as a hedge but lost in §2 of the synthesis). The ICJ has not itself adjudicated whether "fully conclusive" exceeds BRD; the *Bosnia* Court used the formulation without comparison. Schabas treats it as functionally equivalent to BRD in *Genocide in International Law* (2009, pp. 510–520) read carefully, while observing the Court's preference for the distinctive formulation. The synthesis should preserve the hedge.

**Recommendation:** in §2, add: "Whether the 'fully conclusive' standard is materially more demanding than beyond-reasonable-doubt is unresolved (Mettraux 2019; Schabas 2009)."

### M2. The purpose-vs-knowledge intent debate is in agent 01 but disappears from the synthesis.

Agent 01 §3 correctly identifies the Greenawalt knowledge-based reading (Greenawalt, *Columbia L. Rev.* 99, 1999) versus the Schabas purpose-based reading, with Ambos's two-level distinction (purpose at leadership, knowledge at lower-level). The synthesis at §2 reproduces the ICJ purpose-based formulation without ever signalling that there is a doctrinal alternative articulated by serious scholars and a contested middle position (Ambos). The reader is given the ICJ purpose formulation as if it were uncontested.

The check from the brief is also a "no": the executive brief at §2 says the *dolus specialis* is "the specific intent to destroy the group as such, distinct from the intent to commit the underlying acts" — a purpose-based reading, unmarked.

**Recommendation:** the synthesis at §2 should add one sentence: "The ICJ formulation tracks a purpose-based conception of intent; a knowledge-based reading articulated by Greenawalt (1999) and a two-level reading articulated by Ambos (2009) remain in the scholarly conversation but have not been adopted by the Court."

### M3. The "in part" / substantial-part analysis under *Krstić* is reproduced but not applied to the Gaza-as-part question.

The synthesis at §2 reproduces *Krstić* (Appeals Judgment) ¶¶8–14 on numeric, prominence, geographic, and emblematic criteria for "substantial part." It then quotes the ICJ's 26 January 2024 Order at ¶45: "Palestinians in Gaza form a substantial part of that group." That move skips the underlying analytic question that the *Krstić* test poses for any merits adjudication: whether the protected group is "Palestinians" (as the Court treats it at the plausibility stage), and whether Palestinians in Gaza are a substantial part of that broader group on numeric, geographic, or emblematic criteria. The *Bosnia* Court applied the same analysis to Bosnian Muslims at Srebrenica (¶¶198–201) and concluded that the Srebrenica Muslims were a substantial part of the Bosnian Muslim group on geographic and emblematic grounds.

The synthesis is procedurally correct that the Court has made this finding at the plausibility stage. Doctrinally, the merits analysis of "substantial part" under the *Krstić* criteria will be a contested element of any future merits judgment, and the synthesis could flag it without prejudging.

**Recommendation:** in §4 of the synthesis, add a sentence registering that the "substantial part" question at the merits stage will require analysis under the *Krstić* ¶¶12–14 multi-criterion framework, and that Gaza's status as substantial part of the broader Palestinian group is a doctrinal question (number of Palestinians in Gaza relative to Palestinians worldwide; geographic concentration; emblematic significance; the *Bosnia* ¶¶198–201 framework).

### M4. The subjective-vs-objective protected-group debate is in agent 01 but elided in the synthesis.

Agent 01 §5 identifies *Akayesu*'s initial objective approach (¶¶510–516, "stable and permanent") and the trend toward a subjective or mixed standard in *Krstić* (Trial, ¶¶555–557) and *Rutaganda* (¶¶55–58), with Schabas insisting on objective content (Schabas 2009, pp. 124–129). The synthesis at §2 simply notes the Court's plausibility-stage observation about Palestinians and moves on. For a public-publication evidentiary report, that is acceptable; for a doctrinal record, it under-represents the contested status of how the Convention's four categories are identified.

In the Gaza setting specifically, whether Palestinians are best characterised as a "national" group, an "ethnical" group, or both, and whether subjective perpetrator perception or objective group features control the inquiry, are live doctrinal questions. The Court's ¶45 language ("appear to constitute a distinct 'national, ethnical, racial or religious group'") is hedged.

**Recommendation:** add a sentence in §2 of the synthesis registering that the subjective-objective debate (Schabas vs. the *Rutaganda* trend) remains live and that the Court's ¶45 phrasing does not adjudicate it.

### M5. The synthesis's treatment of Article III incitement is incomplete.

The synthesis at §6 reports that the CoI "named the President, Prime Minister, and former Defence Minister as having incited genocide." Direct and public incitement to commit genocide under Article III(c) is a doctrinally distinct offence, requiring no underlying genocide for the incitement itself to be punishable (Article III(c); *Akayesu*, ¶¶551–562; ICTR, *Nahimana et al.* ("Media case"), Trial Judgment, 3 December 2003, ¶¶1010–1029; Appeals Judgment, 28 November 2007, ¶¶692–715). The synthesis treats incitement as a CoI conclusion item without flagging that it is a separately punishable inchoate offence with its own elements (publicness; directness; specific intent to incite genocide). The CoI's invocation of incitement is doctrinally significant in its own right.

**Recommendation:** in §2 or §5(a), add a brief paragraph on Article III(c) as a distinct inchoate offence; note that the CoI's incitement findings are independent of any commission finding.

### M6. The "calculated to bring about" doctrinal content under Article II(c) is undertheorised.

The synthesis at §4(c) and the brief at §4(c) discuss "calculated to bring about physical destruction" without analyzing what "calculated" requires. The drafting history (Schabas 2009, pp. 184–197) and *Krstić* Trial Judgment ¶¶517–518 treat "calculated" as referring to the objective tendency of the conditions to bring about destruction, with the dolus specialis carried in the chapeau of Article II rather than re-instantiated in (c). *Karadžić* (Trial Judgment, 24 March 2016, ¶¶544–548) and *Stakić* (Trial Judgment, 31 July 2003, ¶¶517–518) develop the line.

The synthesis treats "calculated" as collapsing into the dolus specialis discussion ("the calculation behind them goes to *mens rea*," §4(c)). That is one defensible reading; a competing reading distinguishes the "calculated" element as an objective characteristic of the conditions (Schabas 2009, p. 192). The synthesis should either pick a position with citation or register the doctrinal split.

**Recommendation:** in §4(c), register the doctrinal disagreement on whether "calculated" is a separate objective requirement (Schabas; *Krstić* Trial ¶¶517–518) or absorbs into the chapeau dolus specialis.

### M7. The "forcibly transferring" element's connection to group-identity destruction is correctly stated but the doctrinal architecture is thin.

The synthesis at §4(e) correctly notes the drafting-history sense of Article II(e) (absorption into the perpetrator group with intent to destroy source-group identity) and *Akayesu*'s treatment. It does not, however, register that (e) is, in the Convention's structure, the lone act that does not require physical or biological destruction; it captures cultural/identity destruction precisely as a narrow exception to the Convention's otherwise physical/biological focus (Schabas 2009, pp. 201–207; *Krstić* Appeals Judgment, ¶25 (treating cultural destruction as evidence of intent but not as itself a genocidal act)). The synthesis correctly concludes the (e) record is thin; the doctrinal point that (e) is the narrow exception to physical/biological destruction is worth registering.

**Recommendation:** in §4(e), add: "Article II(e) is the only enumerated act that targets group identity directly rather than physical or biological existence; cultural destruction otherwise figures only as evidentiary of intent under Article II's chapeau (*Krstić*, Appeals Judgment, ¶25)."

## 4. LOW-Severity Findings

### L1. The "killing members" actus reus reading could cite *Bosnia* ¶¶276–297.

The synthesis at §4(a) cites *Akayesu* ¶¶500–501 and *Krstić* Appeals ¶34 for the "intentional unlawful homicide" reading. The ICJ's own treatment of "killing" in *Bosnia v. Serbia* ¶¶276–297 (treating the Srebrenica killings as fulfilling (a)) is the more directly controlling authority for ICJ-track analysis and is cited in agent 01 but dropped in the synthesis.

### L2. The "Gambia v. Myanmar" Preliminary Objections finding on erga omnes partes standing is mentioned at §2 but not anchored in the synthesis's structural account.

The synthesis at §2 cites *Gambia v. Myanmar* (Preliminary Objections, 22 July 2022, ¶¶107–114) on erga omnes partes standing. The same case is relevant in *South Africa v. Israel* for confirming South Africa's standing notwithstanding the absence of a direct interest. The brief at §1 does not mention standing; for the public reader, a one-line note that standing was uncontroversial after *Gambia v. Myanmar* would strengthen the §3 ("Judicial Posture") account.

### L3. The "dolus specialis" formulation at *Bosnia* ¶¶186–189 could be paired with *Krstić* Appeals ¶¶32–37.

The synthesis cites *Bosnia* ¶¶186–189 for the dolus specialis architecture. *Krstić* Appeals ¶¶32–37 develops the same architecture at the criminal-responsibility level and is the more familiar source in ICTY jurisprudence. Pairing them strengthens the doctrinal anchor.

### L4. The standard for attributing organ conduct to the State is invoked but not stated.

Agent 01 §4 invokes ILC Articles 4–11; the synthesis at §4(a) repeats the reference but does not state that organ conduct (Art. 4 ARSIWA) is attributable as of right, without need for an "effective control" inquiry (*Bosnia* ¶¶384–395). Because the conduct at issue in *South Africa v. Israel* is overwhelmingly conduct of Israeli State organs (the IDF, COGAT, ministries), Article 4 attributability is uncontroversial and the synthesis's silence is harmless. A one-line statement that organ conduct is attributable per ARSIWA Art. 4 and *Bosnia* ¶¶384–395 would close the doctrinal loop for the reader.

## 6. Doctrinal-Debate Framing Assessment

The brief was instructed to engage four doctrinal debates: purpose-vs-knowledge intent; the "in part" threshold; subjective-vs-objective protected group; plausibility-vs-merits. The assessment:

**Purpose-vs-knowledge intent.** Agent 01 engages this fairly (Greenawalt vs. Schabas vs. Ambos two-level). The synthesis at §2 does not. (M2 above.) For a public-publication evidentiary report, the elision is defensible; for doctrinal completeness, one sentence should register the debate.

**"In part" / substantial-part.** The *Krstić* criteria are reproduced. They are not applied to the Gaza-as-part question (M3 above). The synthesis at §2 collapses into the Court's ¶45 finding without flagging that "substantial part" is a contested merits question.

**Subjective-vs-objective protected group.** Agent 01 engages this. The synthesis does not. (M4 above.)

**Plausibility-vs-merits.** This is the cleanest of the four. The synthesis at §2 expressly distinguishes the procedural plausibility standard from the merits standard. The brief at §2 likewise. Neither blurs.

Overall the doctrinal-debate framing is partial: cleanly done on plausibility-vs-merits; competent on the "in part" doctrine at the standard-recitation level; weaker on subjective-vs-objective group and on purpose-vs-knowledge intent.
