# Mens Rea: Intent Evidence

*Sub-agent 6 output for* Application of the Genocide Convention to the Israel–Palestine Conflict: An Evidentiary Review (as of May 2026).

This document catalogues the evidentiary record relevant to dolus specialis (specific intent) under Article II of the Genocide Convention. It does not adjudicate intent. The synthesis agent (Agent 9) applies the legal standard catalogued in Section 1 to the evidence catalogued in Sections 2 through 5, with the analytic framework in Section 6 as a guide.

## 1. The ICJ standard for intent inference

The Genocide Convention requires proof that prohibited acts were committed "with intent to destroy, in whole or in part, a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" (Article II). The ICJ has held that this specific intent (dolus specialis) may be established in one of two ways.

The first path is direct: explicit statements by responsible officials evidencing intent to destroy the protected group. The second path is inferential: a pattern of conduct from which intent to destroy can be deduced. In *Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Bosnia and Herzegovina v. Serbia and Montenegro)*, the Court held that for a pattern of conduct to be accepted as evidence of specific intent, "it would have to be such that it could only point to the existence of such intent" (Judgment of 26 February 2007, para. 373). The Court repeated and clarified the standard in *Application of the Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide (Croatia v. Serbia)*, Judgment of 3 February 2015, para. 148, holding that the inference must be "the only inference that could reasonably be drawn from the acts in question."

This is a high evidentiary bar by design. A pattern of conduct that produces destruction of the group (in whole or in part) is not, standing alone, sufficient. The destruction must be the only reasonable inference from the conduct. Alternate explanations consistent with the conduct (e.g. counter-insurgency objectives, military necessity, indiscriminate force without specific group-destructive intent) defeat the inference, even where they coexist with the destruction. Scholars including Schabas have argued the standard is unrealistically high in practice; others (Heller, Ambos) have argued the standard is appropriate given the gravity of the charge and the risk of mislabelling other crimes as genocide. The ICJ's January 26, 2024 *Order on Provisional Measures* in *South Africa v. Israel* did not adjudicate intent but found "at least some of the rights claimed by South Africa" under the Genocide Convention "plausible," a procedural threshold distinct from the merits standard above (ICJ, *Order of 26 January 2024*).

## 3. Pattern-of-conduct evidence relevant to intent inference

The SA *Application* (esp. paras. 41–94), the SA *Memorial* (Oct 2024, summary released by DIRCO), Amnesty International's "*You Feel Like You Are Subhuman*" report (Dec 2024), HRW's "*Extermination and Acts of Genocide: Israel Deliberately Depriving Palestinians in Gaza of Water*" (Dec 2024), and the UN Commission of Inquiry's A/HRC/60/CRP.3 (16 Sept 2025) catalogue the following pattern-of-conduct items as cumulatively supporting an intent inference. Each is recorded as catalogued by the source. Agent 9 applies the *Bosnia* / *Croatia* "only reasonable inference" test.

- **Scale and pace of the air campaign, first weeks.** OCHA, UNSAT and HRW figures for Oct–Dec 2023 indicate strike volumes and tonnage exceeding comparable urban campaigns. SA *Application* para. 49. Israel responds that the strike density reflects Hamas's embedding in dense civilian infrastructure.
- **AI-assisted targeting reporting.** +972 / Local Call (3 April 2024) reported on "Lavender" and "Where's Daddy" systems and described the IDF's described practice of permitting up to 15–20 collateral civilian deaths for junior Hamas operatives, citing six anonymous Israeli intelligence officers. The IDF Spokesperson rebutted: Lavender is "a database used to cross-reference intelligence sources ... not a system" determining strikes; the article's characterisation reflects "a flawed understanding of IDF directives and international law." Cited by Amnesty and the UN CoI; included in the SA *Memorial*.
- **"Complete siege" announcement and operational implementation.** Gallant's 9 Oct 2023 announcement (Section 2) was followed by extended restrictions on water, electricity and fuel. HRW's December 2024 report concludes that the deliberate water-deprivation pattern itself constitutes acts of genocide and extermination. Israel responds that aid restrictions reflected security review of dual-use risks and that humanitarian flows were facilitated (Section 4).
- **Restrictions on humanitarian access.** OCHA, WFP and IPC reporting through 2024 and 2025 documenting famine conditions; Agent 4 catalogues this evidence. The SA *Application* and *Memorial* frame restrictions as conditions of life calculated to bring physical destruction (Article II(c)).
- **Statements by frontline soldiers and social media content.** The CoI and Amnesty have analysed which soldier statements are representative versus isolated. Amnesty's Dec 2024 report includes a non-exhaustive set of geolocated and verified videos of IDF personnel celebrating destruction of homes, mosques and infrastructure. Israel has stated that such statements do not reflect orders or policy and that some have led to disciplinary review.
- **Destruction of cultural property, archives, universities, cemeteries.** UNESCO has verified damage to 164 cultural sites since 7 October 2023 (as of March 2026), including 14 religious sites, 128 buildings of historical/artistic interest, 9 monuments, 2 museums and 8 archaeological sites. UNESCO records that 22 of 38 university campuses are completely destroyed and 14 more sustained damage. Cited by the SA *Memorial* as pattern evidence of intent to destroy the group "as such."
- **Civilian infrastructure destruction patterns.** UNOSAT damage assessments (multiple, 2024–2025) and the HRW water-system report document systematic destruction of bakeries, water and sanitation infrastructure, agricultural land, hospitals, and housing stock. Cited by Amnesty and CoI as cumulative pattern evidence. Israel's *Counter-Memorial* (filed 12 March 2026) is reported to argue these reflect Hamas use of civilian infrastructure for military purposes and the operational necessity of degrading military capacity.

## 4. Counter-intent evidence

Items the Israeli government, its ICJ legal team and supportive scholarship cite as inconsistent with genocidal intent. Recorded here as catalogued by Israel's pleadings, IDF/COGAT publications and supportive scholars. Agent 9 weighs these against the Section 3 record under the "only reasonable inference" standard.

- **Evacuation warnings.** IDF Spokesperson records of leaflet drops, SMS messages, recorded phone calls and pre-strike "knock" procedures across Oct 2023 through 2025. Israel's January 2024 oral pleadings (Galit Raguan) presented these as evidence of distinction. Critics (including the UN CoI, Amnesty) respond that evacuation directions to areas that were themselves struck and were unreachable due to fuel/road conditions undermine the protective intent claim.
- **Humanitarian corridor openings.** IDF/COGAT records of declared corridors (Salah al-Din, coastal route) during designated windows. Israel's pleadings cite these. The CoI and Amnesty report that the corridors were repeatedly closed, struck or made impassable.
- **Polio vaccination cooperation.** The WHO–COGAT preliminary agreement (29 Aug 2024) and the two-round polio vaccination campaign (Sept and Oct–Nov 2024) administered 1.1 million doses, achieving approximately 90 percent coverage of Gaza children, per IDF and WHO reporting. Cited by Israel's pleadings and supportive scholars (e.g. CFR analysis, Nov 2024) as inconsistent with intent to destroy the group.
- **Coordination with Egypt on Rafah crossing.** Documented in COGAT releases through May 2024 (closure of Rafah after the IDF Rafah operation altered this pattern).
- **Aid trucks facilitated (COGAT figures).** COGAT reported over 33,882 aid trucks entered Gaza from January through end of July 2025; over one million tons of food across 2025. UN OCHA figures are materially lower; the gap (reported at ~6,000 trucks) is itself contested. The SA *Memorial* cites the UN figures as more reliable.
- **Statements by Israeli officials affirming distinction.** PMO and IDF Spokesperson statements (multiple) affirming IHL compliance, including Halevi statements (Section 2). Israel's *Counter-Memorial* lists these as the relevant policy speech, framing Section 2's listed statements as political rhetoric not operationalised through orders.
- **Field hospital establishments.** UAE field hospital at Tel al-Sultan; American floating pier (March–July 2024); Jordanian field hospital. Israel cites facilitation of these; humanitarian organisations have reported access friction and short-lived operation.
- **Stated war aim.** Israel's pleadings frame the operational objective as (i) destruction of Hamas's military and governance capacity, (ii) recovery of hostages taken on 7 October 2023, (iii) prevention of recurrence of attacks of the 7 October type. Israel argues the stated and operationalised aim is destruction of Hamas as an armed organisation, not Palestinians as a protected group, and that this is consistent with armed conflict aims rather than genocidal intent under Article II.
- **Sanctions / suspensions of officials.** Netanyahu's suspension of Eliyahu from cabinet meetings (Nov 2023). Attorney General Baharav-Miara's statement (10 Jan 2024) that incitement cases were under review.

## 6. Scholarly framework on rhetoric vs. policy distinction

Scholars analyse the gap between political rhetoric and operationalised intent under three frames recognisable in the ICTY/ICTR and ICJ jurisprudence.

**Schabas.** In *Genocide in International Law* (2nd ed. 2009) and subsequent writings (including 2024–2025 interviews and Oxford blog posts), Schabas has argued that statements by senior officials must be considered together with conduct, and that policy can be inferred from a combination of explicit statements and pattern of acts. Schabas has also argued the ICJ's "only reasonable inference" standard is stringent and that international courts have set a higher bar for inference than ICTR practice in cases such as *Akayesu* and *Kayishema*. In 2024 commentary, Schabas stated that the evidence in *South Africa v. Israel* "more than just a pattern of conduct" combined with "statements and clear indications of policy" warrants serious examination.

**Heller.** In writings on Opinio Juris and Just Security (2023–2025), Kevin Jon Heller has argued that the "only reasonable inference" standard is the controlling formulation and that statements which are repudiated, sanctioned or made by officials lacking command authority carry materially less weight in intent inference. Heller has cautioned against equating widespread inflammatory rhetoric with legally cognisable intent.

**Akhavan.** Payam Akhavan, in *Reducing Genocide to Law* (Cambridge, 2012) and contemporary commentary, has emphasised that the dolus specialis threshold was designed to be narrow and that pattern-of-conduct inference must rigorously exclude alternative explanations such as counter-insurgency objectives and indiscriminate but not specifically destructive intent. Akhavan has argued the policy of starvation and conditions of life evidence in Gaza poses serious questions under Article II(c).

**Ambos.** Kai Ambos has written that the "only reasonable inference" standard is strict but not impossible, and has analysed in detail the difference between joint criminal enterprise (JCE)-style attribution of statements to a collective and the narrower requirement that the genocidal intent be held by specific perpetrators. Ambos has noted that statements without command authority do not establish state intent without independent evidence of operational adoption.

**Greenawalt.** Alexander Greenawalt's "knowledge-based" approach to genocidal intent (Columbia Law Review, 1999) has been engaged with by the ICJ and ICC obliquely but not formally adopted. The standard remains intent-based.

**Bartov and Segal.** As historians of genocide (cited in Agent 1's framework), Omer Bartov and Raz Segal have separately concluded in 2023–2024 commentary that the public statements record, taken with the pattern of conduct, meets a threshold of intent in their view; both note this is a historian's not a court's standard. Both have published in major outlets (Guardian, NY Review, +972) with primary-source citation.

The framework relevant to this catalogue is: (i) statements by speakers with direct command authority are weighted more heavily than statements by speakers without; (ii) statements directly tied to operationalised orders are weighted more heavily than political rhetoric; (iii) repudiation, sanction or prosecution of a statement reduces its weight as state intent; failure to sanction increases the weight; (iv) pattern-of-conduct evidence must exclude alternative reasonable inferences (counter-insurgency, indiscriminate force, conditions of conflict). The bar is high by design. The SA *Application* argues the volume, seniority, consistency and non-repudiation of statements, combined with the conduct, meet this bar. Israel responds that the operational war aim has been the destruction of Hamas's military and governance capacity and recovery of hostages, and that this is the only inference consistent with the orders that were issued and the war as conducted. Agent 9 applies the legal test to the evidence catalogued here and in Sections 3 through 5 of the broader report.
