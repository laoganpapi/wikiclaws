# Red Team 17 (Academic Genocide Studies): Peer-Review Memo

*Reviewer position: tenured genocide-studies / international-criminal-law scholar, in the disciplinary tradition of Bartov, Schabas, Heller, Ambos, Moses, Goldberg, Shaw, Mann, O'Brien, Quigley, and the Lemkin Institute leadership. Reviewing for fit at a major peer-reviewed venue (Holocaust and Genocide Studies, Journal of Genocide Research, International Journal of Transitional Justice, European Journal of International Law, Leiden Journal of International Law). Reviewing date: 25 May 2026.*

---

## 1. Executive Summary

The synthesis (`09_synthesis_output.md`) and brief (`10_executive_brief_output.md`) are evidentiarily strong, doctrinally careful on the ICJ standard, and unusually scrupulous in declining to issue a verdict. As a public-publication artifact, the work is above the median of what circulates under journalistic or NGO imprint. As a candidate for submission to a major peer-reviewed venue in genocide studies or international criminal law, it would not, in this reviewer's judgment, clear the bar without major revisions. The shortfalls are not in the documentation: they are in the engagement with the field's internal debates and with the named scholars whose tradition the work invokes. The synthesis names roughly thirty scholars in two summary lists, but engages substantively with perhaps four. The substantial-part jurisprudence is gestured at; the qualitative-vs-quantitative debate is absent. Bartov's and Schabas's evolutions are treated as citations rather than as the methodological case studies they actually are in the discipline. The settler-colonialism frame (Wolfe, Veracini, Erakat, Rashed/Short, Pappé) is wholly absent, even where the report relies on scholars who deploy it. The politicide / ethnocide / cultural-genocide alternative-category literature (Harff, Shaw, Moses) is not engaged. Comparative jurisprudence beyond ICJ Bosnia and ICTY Krstić (specifically Karadžić, Tolimir, Stakić, and the Myanmar IIFFM intent reasoning) is missing. The IAGS resolution is reported with its turnout caveat but without the methodological reflection the field requires. None of these gaps rises to a reason to reject; collectively they read as a synthesis written by competent generalists rather than by a scholar embedded in the discipline.

**Verdict: major revision.** A major-revision recommendation, in this reviewer's hands, is the most common outcome for substantive submissions and signals that the work has the underlying material to succeed but requires re-engagement with the field's debates before it would survive peer review at a journal of record.

Severity counts: HIGH 6, MEDIUM 7, LOW 5.

## 2. HIGH-Severity Findings

### H1. The Bosnia / Croatia jurisprudence is truncated; Karadžić, Tolimir, and Stakić are absent

The synthesis cites *Krstić* (Trial 2001, Appeal 2004), *Bosnia v. Serbia* (2007), and *Croatia v. Serbia* (2015), and lifts the controlling ¶373 / ¶148 formulation. That is the floor. The field expects engagement with the ICTY's further development of the doctrine: *Prosecutor v. Karadžić* (Trial Chamber 24 March 2016; Appeals Chamber 20 March 2019), which upheld the genocide conviction for Srebrenica while acquitting on the broader Bosnia count and which produced the most detailed appellate treatment of pattern-of-conduct intent inference since *Krstić*; *Prosecutor v. Tolimir* (Trial Chamber 12 December 2012; Appeals Chamber 8 April 2015), which addressed the destruction of group leadership and the "substantial part" element with explicit qualitative reasoning; and *Prosecutor v. Stakić* (Trial Chamber 31 July 2003; Appeals Chamber 22 March 2006), which the Appeals Chamber used to clarify what does not amount to genocidal intent. The Karadžić appellate framing of how broader-conflict intent claims fail even where localized intent is established is the most analytically relevant precedent for any application of the Convention to Gaza, and the synthesis does not cite it. *Bosnia v. Serbia* is invoked only for the high-threshold formulation; the analytically prior question of how the Court reached its bifurcated finding (genocide at Srebrenica, no genocide for the wider conflict) is not engaged. This is the central comparative-jurisprudence question for a Gaza analysis, because it is precisely the question whether destruction of a geographically defined sub-set of the protected group can support an Article II finding without the wider-conflict finding. Schabas, *Genocide in International Law* (Cambridge UP, 2nd ed. 2009), Chapters 4 and 5; Mark Drumbl, *Atrocity, Punishment, and International Law* (Cambridge UP, 2007); Marko Milanović, "State Responsibility for Genocide" (EJIL 17:3, 2006) and "State Responsibility for Genocide: A Follow-Up" (EJIL 18:4, 2007); and Antonio Cassese, "On the Use of Criminal Law Notions in Determining State Responsibility for Genocide" (JICJ 5:4, 2007) would be the natural touchstones.

### H2. *Gambia v. Myanmar* is used only procedurally; the most analytically comparable case is squandered

The synthesis cites *Gambia v. Myanmar* twice (the Preliminary Objections of 22 July 2022 for erga omnes partes; the Provisional Measures Order of 23 January 2020 for the plausibility framework). It does not engage with the Independent International Fact-Finding Mission on Myanmar (A/HRC/39/64, 12 September 2018; A/HRC/42/CRP.5, 16 September 2019; A/HRC/42/50, 8 August 2019), which produced the most analytically structured public application of the Convention to a contemporary State conduct since the post-Yugoslav tribunals. The IIFFM reasoning on inference of genocidal intent from pattern of conduct, on the "in part" threshold relative to a sub-group within a sub-state geographic area (Rohingya in Rakhine State), and on the Tatmadaw command structure is the closest analytical analog to the Gaza application. Melanie O'Brien (whom the synthesis lists at row (a) without engagement) has published on this directly: O'Brien, "'Don't kill them, let's choose them as wives': The development of the crimes of forced marriage, sexual slavery and enforced prostitution in international criminal law" (IJTJ 10:3, 2016) and her 2024 work as IAGS President. Priya Pillai, Michael Becker (*The Plight of the Rohingya*, EJIL: Talk! and JICJ contributions, 2020), and Adil Ahmad Haque on the *Gambia* plausibility test all belong here. The synthesis's failure to use Myanmar as the analytically dominant comparator (rather than as a procedural footnote) is the single largest scholarly weakness in the work.

### H3. The Bartov and Schabas evolutions are reported as citations, not as methodological case studies

Bartov's October–November 2023 *New York Times* op-ed ("What I Believe as a Historian of Genocide," 10 November 2023, conclusion: war crimes and crimes against humanity, not yet genocide), his 13 August 2024 *NYT Magazine* essay ("As a Former IDF Soldier and Historian of Genocide, I Was Deeply Disturbed by My Recent Visit to Israel," with the operative "inescapable conclusion" language already present in qualified form), and his 15 July 2025 *NYT* essay (full "inescapable conclusion") are three points on a methodological trajectory. The synthesis cites only the July 2025 endpoint. This matters because the disciplinary significance of Bartov's position is precisely the trajectory: a Holocaust historian working from the methodological tradition of *Anatomy of a Genocide: The Life and Death of a Town Called Buczacz* (Simon & Schuster, 2018) and *Erased: Vanishing Traces of Jewish Galicia in Present-Day Ukraine* (Princeton UP, 2007) reaching the genocide threshold against his own evident reluctance. The methodological weight of that trajectory is not transferable to the endpoint citation alone.

The Schabas case is parallel and more consequential because Schabas is the leading Anglophone genocide-law treatise author and was historically the most vocal "preserve the categories" skeptic of expansive Convention application. Schabas, *Genocide in International Law: The Crime of Crimes* (Cambridge UP, 1st ed. 2000; 2nd ed. 2009; 3rd ed. forthcoming), Chapter 1 on the protected groups and Chapter 6 on dolus specialis, has shaped a generation. His 2024–2025 statements ("arguably the strongest case of genocide that has ever come before the Court," ECPS interview; Anadolu Agency 2025; Oxford Centre for Criminology Blog, July 2025) are an evolution from a position the field reads as the conservative pole. The synthesis registers the statements without registering what the evolution costs Schabas in his own intellectual genealogy and what the disciplinary signal is when the field's leading skeptic moves. A scholar reviewer would expect at minimum a paragraph on what Schabas's earlier work argued, what his current position argues, and what changed.

### H4. The "intent vs. motive" distinction is collapsed

The Convention's "as such" qualifier and the Schabas / Akhavan / Greenawalt literature on the intent-motive distinction are central to any serious dolus specialis analysis. Schabas, *Genocide in International Law*, 2nd ed., pp. 244–256, distinguishes intent (the requisite mental state directed at the destruction of the group) from motive (the underlying reason: revenge, security, ideology, territory). Payam Akhavan, *Reducing Genocide to Law* (Cambridge UP, 2012), develops the same distinction. Alexander K. A. Greenawalt's "Rethinking Genocidal Intent: The Case for a Knowledge-Based Interpretation" (Columbia Law Review 99:8, 1999) advances the knowledge-based alternative; Kai Ambos, "What does 'intent to destroy' in genocide mean?" (International Review of the Red Cross 91:876, 2009) defends a structured purpose-based reading. Kevin Jon Heller, "The Three Accountability Mechanisms" (in Kevin Jon Heller and Markus D. Dubber eds., *The Handbook of Comparative Criminal Law*, Stanford UP, 2011) and his EJIL: Talk! commentary across 2024–2025 are the contemporary applications. The synthesis cites Heller and Ambos in its sources list but does not engage with the purpose-vs-knowledge debate at the level the question requires. The "as such" element is mentioned in passing but is not treated as the analytically distinct doctrinal step it is: destruction of the group qua group, not destruction of individuals who happen to belong to the group. Israeli pleadings make a motive-style argument (counterterrorism objective directed at Hamas, not at the group as such). Whether that argument is a motive argument (and therefore irrelevant under Schabas's reading) or an intent argument (and therefore directly responsive) is the live doctrinal question. The synthesis flattens it.

### H5. The settler-colonialism frame is wholly absent

A substantial body of scholarly literature applies the settler-colonialism analytic to Israel-Palestine: Patrick Wolfe, "Settler Colonialism and the Elimination of the Native" (Journal of Genocide Research 8:4, 2006); Lorenzo Veracini, *Israel and Settler Society* (Pluto, 2006) and *Settler Colonialism: A Theoretical Overview* (Palgrave, 2010); Noura Erakat, *Justice for Some: Law and the Question of Palestine* (Stanford UP, 2019); Mohammed Bamyeh and others. The frame has direct implications for the genocide analysis: (i) it reframes the temporal scope (not 7 October 2023 onward but 1948 / 1967 onward); (ii) it reframes the protected-group analysis (Palestinians as such vs. Palestinians in Gaza as a substantial part); (iii) it reframes the intent analysis (elimination as a structural logic rather than as a discrete mental state). A. Dirk Moses, *The Problems of Genocide: Permanent Security and the Language of Transgression* (Cambridge UP, 2021), is the disciplinary bridge: Moses argues that the Convention's category structure occludes structural elimination logics, and his work is directly relevant. Martin Shaw, *What Is Genocide?* (Polity, 2nd ed. 2015) and his 2024–2025 contributions on Gaza, makes the same move. Rashed, Short, and Docker, "Nakba Memoricide: Genocide Studies and the Zionist/Israeli Genocide of Palestine" (Holy Land Studies 13:1, 2014), is the direct application. None of these scholars or works is cited. The synthesis lists Erakat in the row (a) catalogue without engagement. A serious peer reviewer would flag the absence as a frame-blindness: the report can decline to adopt the settler-colonial frame, but cannot ignore it, because several of the named scholars in the row (a) list (Erakat, Moses, Shaw, Pappé) reach their conclusions via that frame and the analytical weight of their endorsement of "genocide" depends on it.

### H6. The Holocaust-historian methodological tradition is invoked but not theorized

The synthesis cites Bartov, Goldberg, Mordechai, Segal, Auron, Blatman, Lederman, and Weizman. Several of these (Bartov, Goldberg, Auron, Blatman, Lederman, Segal) operate from a methodological tradition rooted explicitly in Holocaust historiography. The disciplinary significance of Holocaust historians applying the methodology of Holocaust historiography (close reading of perpetrator statements; chain-of-command analysis; document-led process tracing; comparative atrocity analysis) to contemporary Gaza is itself a scholarly contribution and itself contested within the discipline. Yehuda Bauer's tradition (*Rethinking the Holocaust*, Yale UP, 2001) is more cautious about comparative application. Saul Friedländer's tradition is more open. The synthesis does not engage with which Holocaust-historian methodological tradition the named scholars operate from, and does not register that "Holocaust historians have concluded X" is not a flat scholarly category but a contested methodological move that the field reads with care. The Lee Mordechai documentation project (Hebrew University) and the Bartov / Goldberg statements are doing methodologically distinct things. A peer reviewer in this discipline would want that distinction surfaced.

## 3. MEDIUM-Severity Findings

### M1. The substantial-part jurisprudence is light

The *Krstić* Appeals Judgment (¶¶8–14) is cited for the four criteria (numeric, prominence, geographic, emblematic), but the qualitative-vs-quantitative debate is not developed. The Tolimir Trial Chamber's reasoning on the qualitative dimension (leadership destruction), the Karadžić Trial Chamber's reasoning on geographic delimitation, and the *Bosnia v. Serbia* Court's treatment of the Bosnian Muslims of Eastern Bosnia as a substantial part (¶¶198–201) are not engaged. Schabas's treatment of the substantial-part question (2nd ed., pp. 277–286) and his frequently quoted line that "a court may regard a relatively small part as substantial if the destruction of that part is significant for the survival of the whole" is the analytic anchor. Applying this to Gaza specifically: 2.1 million Palestinians in Gaza, of a global Palestinian population of approximately 14 million; the geographic-and-emblematic argument that Gaza is the densest concentration and the most politically symbolic component of the Palestinian people. None of this is in the synthesis. The brief simply cites the four criteria.

### M2. The Lemkin genealogy and Convention drafting history are glancingly mentioned

The synthesis references the "drafting history" once (in connection with Article II(e)). The Lemkin genealogy is not discussed: Raphael Lemkin, *Axis Rule in Occupied Europe* (Carnegie Endowment, 1944), Chapter IX; Lemkin's drafting role at the UN through 1947–1948; the 1948 negotiations and the political-groups exclusion (Schabas 2009, pp. 117–150; Anton Weiss-Wendt, *A Rhetorical Crime: Genocide in the Geopolitical Discourse of the Cold War*, Rutgers UP, 2018; A. Dirk Moses, *The Problems of Genocide*). The genealogical question matters because Lemkin's original conception was broader than the Convention's enacted text, and the field is in active debate about whether the contemporary application should be read back toward Lemkin's broader conception or held to the Convention's narrower enacted scope. Douglas Irvin-Erickson, *Raphaël Lemkin and the Concept of Genocide* (Penn Press, 2017), is the standard recent treatment. The synthesis's treatment is shallow.

### M3. The IAGS resolution context is reported without the methodological reflection

The synthesis correctly notes the IAGS resolution of 31 August 2025 passed at 86% of voting members of approximately 28% turnout, with several hundred scholars subsequently signing a retraction request. What the synthesis does not register is what this means for the disciplinary weight of the resolution as evidence. The methodology of scholarly-association resolutions and their epistemic status (Bauer's longstanding caution; the IAGS internal divisions traceable to the Armenian and Bosnia resolution controversies of the 1990s and 2000s; Helen Fein's work on the sociology of genocide-studies institutions; Israel Charny's role in the field's formation) frames how a peer reviewer reads "IAGS resolved X." A peer reviewer in genocide studies would expect the synthesis to register that the IAGS is one institution within a contested field, that its resolutions historically have been criticized for methodological under-specification, and that the resolution is a disciplinary signal rather than a finding. The synthesis gets the empirics right but does not contextualize them.

### M4. Politicide, ethnocide, and the alternative-category literature is absent

Barbara Harff and Ted Robert Gurr, "Toward Empirical Theory of Genocides and Politicides" (International Studies Quarterly 32:3, 1988); Harff, "No Lessons Learned from the Holocaust? Assessing Risks of Genocide and Political Mass Murder since 1955" (American Political Science Review 97:1, 2003); Helen Fein, *Genocide: A Sociological Perspective* (Sage, 1993); Martin Shaw, *What Is Genocide?* (Polity, 2nd ed. 2015); A. Dirk Moses on "permanent security" as an alternative analytic category; the ethnocide / cultural-genocide literature (Lawrence Davidson, *Cultural Genocide*, Rutgers UP, 2012). Some serious scholars hold that the Gaza conduct is better captured by politicide (destruction of a political community without falling within the Article II protected groups), by ethnocide / cultural genocide (destruction of group identity without the physical-destruction predicate), or by extermination as a crime against humanity (the ICC's chosen frame). The synthesis does not engage with the alternative-category position. The brief does engage with the ICC's extermination charge but does not flag that some scholars argue this is the analytically correct frame and the Article II framing is conceptually mismatched.

### M5. The ICJ Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024 is undertheorized

The synthesis cites the Advisory Opinion of 19 July 2024 (*Legal Consequences arising from the Policies and Practices of Israel in the Occupied Palestinian Territory*) for the dispositif paragraph 285(3) on the unlawfulness of continued presence. What the synthesis does not engage with is the Court's findings on systematic discriminatory practices (paragraphs 178–229) and the apartheid-adjacent reasoning, which several scholars (John Dugard, John Reynolds, *Empire, Emergency and International Law*, Cambridge UP, 2017) have read as bearing on the dolus specialis inference. The Advisory Opinion is not a genocide proceeding but its discriminatory-practices findings are scholarly material for the intent inference question. The synthesis flags this in a single sentence and moves on.

### M6. Albanese's institutional standing is conflated with the Commission of Inquiry's

The synthesis correctly distinguishes the UN Commission of Inquiry (Pillay, Sidoti, Kothari) from the Special Rapporteur (Albanese). It does not engage with the doctrinal difference: a UN Special Rapporteur is an independent expert reporting to the Human Rights Council with the institutional weight of mandate-based independent expertise; a Commission of Inquiry is an HRC-mandated fact-finding body with team-based methodology, secretariat resources, and a typically narrower-and-deeper output. The Commission's institutional weight in UN human-rights architecture is greater than the Special Rapporteur's. The synthesis lists both at row (a) without registering the institutional differential. Peer reviewers would expect a paragraph distinguishing the two. The Bell, Brett, and Ramcharan literature on UN fact-finding (notably Bertrand Ramcharan, *International Peace Conferences*, Brill, 2015; and the OHCHR's Commissions of Inquiry and Fact-Finding Missions: Guidance and Practice, 2015) is the methodological anchor.

### M7. The Rwanda jurisprudential foundations are cited but undertheorized

*Akayesu* is cited for elements (a) through (d). *Kayishema and Ruzindana* is cited once for the (b) threshold. The Rwandan tribunal's jurisprudential foundations for protected-group identification (*Akayesu* ¶¶510–516 on the four enumerated categories and the "stable and permanent" reading) and for the use of contextual evidence to infer intent (*Akayesu* ¶¶523, 728) are the analytical foundation for the ICJ's contemporary doctrine. The synthesis treats these as procedural citations rather than as the doctrinal source. The William Schabas, Larissa van den Herik, and Claus Kreß literature (Kreß, "The Crime of Genocide under International Law" (ICLR 6:4, 2006) and "The International Court of Justice and the Elements of the Crime of Genocide" (EJIL 18:4, 2007)) is the standard treatment.

## 4. LOW-Severity Findings

### L1. The "as such" element should be analytically distinguished

The synthesis treats "intent to destroy ... a national, ethnical, racial or religious group, as such" as a single phrase. The "as such" qualifier has its own jurisprudence (Schabas 2009, pp. 256–263; *Stakić* Appeals Judgment ¶¶20–28). One sentence in the legal-standard section would suffice.

### L2. The Auron, Blatman, Lederman roster could be deepened

Israeli scholars in the row (a) position carry disciplinary weight beyond their numbers, and the synthesis would benefit from a sentence on what their named contributions are (Auron, *The Banality of Indifference: Zionism and the Armenian Genocide*, Transaction, 2000; Blatman's commentary; Lederman's work on Holocaust memory and Gaza).

### L3. The Quigley citation could be deepened

John Quigley, *The Genocide Convention: An International Law Analysis* (Ashgate, 2006) is the standard Anglophone treatise alternative to Schabas. The synthesis names Quigley in the row (a) catalogue without engagement with his treatise position.

### L4. The Akhavan position should be developed

The synthesis notes "no formal personal determination identified" for Payam Akhavan. Akhavan, *Reducing Genocide to Law* (Cambridge UP, 2012), is the standard contemporary treatment of dolus specialis from a former ICTY prosecutor's perspective. His silence is itself a disciplinary signal worth a sentence.

### L5. The Lancet Khatib correspondence framing is technically imprecise

The synthesis notes the Khatib, McKee, and Yusuf *Lancet* correspondence (5 July 2024) as suggesting 186,000 conflict-attributable mortality. The correspondence framed this as an order-of-magnitude estimate using a 4:1 indirect-to-direct ratio derived from prior conflict literature; the synthesis correctly notes the framing. Scholarly accuracy would be served by noting that the 4:1 ratio is contested in the public-health literature (see the response correspondence in *Lancet* through late 2024). This is empirical and probably outside this red team's lane (Red Team 15) but is worth flagging where empirical methodology intersects with scholarly debate.

## 5. Items Considered and Dismissed

### D1. The ICJ "fully conclusive" standard is correctly stated

The synthesis correctly states the merits standard of proof from *Bosnia* ¶209 and reaffirmed in *Croatia* ¶178. The framing is doctrinally accurate.

### D2. The "only reasonable inference" formulation is correctly stated

The *Bosnia* ¶373 and *Croatia* ¶148 formulations are accurately quoted, and the *Croatia* ¶¶437–441 application (where two reasonable inferences are available, the genocide inference cannot be drawn) is correctly invoked.

### D3. The plausibility threshold is correctly distinguished from merits

The synthesis is meticulous on the procedural distinction between plausibility (provisional measures) and the merits standard. The ¶30 caveat from the 26 January 2024 Order is correctly cited. This is the single point where the work would survive a doctrinal peer reviewer (Red Team 14) without revision.

### D4. The symmetric Hamas analysis is handled adequately

Section 5(e) on Hamas conduct under symmetric Article II analysis is unusually careful for a public-facing report. The 1988 Charter vs. 2017 Document treatment is balanced, and the structural observation that the legal questions in the two directions are differently posed (State responsibility under Article IX for one side; individual criminal responsibility under Article IV / Rome Statute 25 for the other) is doctrinally correct.

### D5. The five-row determinations matrix is methodologically sound

The Agent 8 / Section 6 determinations matrix's distinction between row (a) "genocide is occurring," row (b) "risk of / plausibly," row (c) "acts of genocide without overall determination," row (d) "serious IHL/IHRL short of genocide," and row (e) "rejects" is the methodologically correct way to record disagreement without arbitrating it. Peer reviewers in this field would not object.

### D6. The Article II(e) restraint is appropriate

The synthesis correctly identifies Article II(e) as thin in the Gaza setting under the Convention's drafting history and *Akayesu* reading. The temptation to inflate child-detention or family-separation data into (e) territory is resisted. This is correct.

## 6. Peer-Review Verdict

**Recommendation: major revision.**

Referee comment (in the form a Holocaust and Genocide Studies / Journal of Genocide Research / IJTJ submission would receive):

> The manuscript addresses a question of substantial disciplinary interest with evidentiary care unusual for public-facing work on Gaza. The doctrinal treatment of the ICJ standard (Bosnia ¶373; Croatia ¶148; provisional measures plausibility; merits "fully conclusive evidence") is accurate. The five-row determinations matrix is methodologically sound. The Article II element-by-element documentation is dense and appropriately cautious on II(d) and II(e). The synthesis correctly resists issuing a verdict where the bodies with formal authority have not.
>
> The manuscript would not, however, survive peer review in its current form. The scholarly weaknesses are concentrated in six areas. First, the Bosnia / Croatia jurisprudence is truncated: Karadžić, Tolimir, and Stakić are absent, and the Bosnia v. Serbia bifurcated finding (genocide at Srebrenica; no genocide for the wider conflict) is the most analytically relevant precedent for a Gaza application and is not engaged. Second, Gambia v. Myanmar is cited procedurally; the IIFFM intent reasoning is the closest contemporary analog and is unused. Third, the Bartov and Schabas evolutions are reported as endpoint citations rather than as the methodological case studies they are; the disciplinary weight of "the field's leading skeptic has moved" requires engagement with what he moved from. Fourth, the intent-vs-motive distinction (Schabas, Akhavan, Greenawalt, Ambos, Heller) is flattened: the Israeli pleadings' counterterrorism framing is treated as an intent argument when on Schabas's reading it is a motive argument, and the doctrinal step is not taken. Fifth, the settler-colonialism frame (Wolfe, Veracini, Erakat, Moses, Shaw, Rashed/Short) is wholly absent, even where the manuscript relies on scholars (Erakat, Moses, Shaw, Pappé) whose conclusions are reached through it. Sixth, the Holocaust-historian methodological tradition is invoked through citation but is not theorized as a methodological move with its own contested status within the discipline.
>
> Medium-severity revisions are needed on the substantial-part jurisprudence (qualitative-quantitative debate, Krstić ¶¶8–14 applied to Gaza), the Lemkin genealogy and Convention drafting history (Irvin-Erickson, Moses, Weiss-Wendt), the IAGS resolution contextualization (the disciplinary history of association resolutions; Helen Fein on the sociology of the field), the alternative-category literature (Harff politicide; Shaw on the conceptual scope; Davidson on cultural genocide), the 19 July 2024 Advisory Opinion's discriminatory-practices findings, the institutional differential between Special Rapporteurs and Commissions of Inquiry, and the Rwanda jurisprudential foundations.
>
> The manuscript's strengths are real: the evidentiary documentation, the doctrinal accuracy on the controlling ICJ standard, the symmetric treatment of Hamas conduct, the five-row determinations matrix, and the appropriate restraint on Article II(e). With major revision addressing the six high-severity gaps and the seven medium-severity gaps, the manuscript would be a publishable contribution. As currently drafted, it is a competent generalist synthesis rather than a discipline-internal contribution, and at a journal of record in this field it would require revision before a second-round review.

## 7. Methodological Note

This memo stays in scholarly-rigor lane. Citation accuracy (Red Team 13) and empirical methodology (Red Team 15) are touched only where they intersect with scholarly debate (L5 above). The reviewer does not opine on whether the conduct in Gaza is or is not genocide; that question is the question the bodies with formal authority have addressed differently and on which the manuscript itself does not adjudicate. The reviewer does opine on whether the manuscript engages with the field's internal debates at the level a major peer-reviewed venue would require. It does not, at present, but the underlying material to do so is in the upstream outputs (Agents 1–8) and the revision is achievable.
